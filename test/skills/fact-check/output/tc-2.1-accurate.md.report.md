## Verdict for C1: "The 2020 US Census counted 331.4 million people."

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

The Census Bureau's April 26, 2021 release gives the resident population on April 1, 2020 as 331,449,281, which rounds to 331.4 million. That was up 7.4% from 308,745,538 in 2010. The Census Bureau is the only body that produces this count, and its other pages restate the same figure, so they don't count as a second independent source. That limits the rating to Medium. I only read the figure in search excerpts of the Bureau's pages, which also caps the rating at Medium. The excerpt states the exact number, so I accepted the cap. A second, separately produced primary record, such as the official count the President sent to Congress, could raise this to High.

One related number to be aware of: the apportionment population is 331,108,434. That figure covers the 50 states only and adds their residents living overseas. It is not the 331.4 million figure (see C2).

**Sources:** U.S. Census Bureau, "2020 Census Apportionment Results Delivered to the President" (press release, 2021); U.S. Census Bureau, America Counts story (2021)
**Citation:** https://census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2021/2020-census-apportionment-results.html#:~:text=331,449,281

## Verdict for C2: "This decennial count forms the basis for congressional apportionment"

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Article I, Section 2, Clause 3 of the Constitution (as amended by the Fourteenth Amendment, Section 2) requires an "actual Enumeration" every ten years and uses it to divide House seats among the states. The Census Bureau calls apportionment the census's original legal purpose. There are two independent primary sources here (the Constitution and the Bureau), but I read both only at excerpt level, so the rating is capped at Medium.

One detail: apportionment uses the 331,108,434 apportionment population (50 states plus overseas residents), not the full 331.4 million resident count, which also includes DC. The decennial count is still the basis for apportionment, as the draft says.

**Sources:** U.S. Constitution, Art. I §2 cl. 3, via Cornell LII; U.S. Census Bureau, "Census in the Constitution" and "About Congressional Apportionment"; U.S. Census Bureau, America Counts story (2021)
**Citation:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/article-1/section-2/clause-3/enumeration-clause-and-apportioning-seats-in-the-house-of-representatives

## Verdict for C3: "…and federal funding allocation."

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

A 2023 Census Bureau report found that in fiscal year 2021, 353 federal assistance listings used decennial census data, in whole or in part, to distribute more than $2.8 trillion. The Bureau notes that it doesn't set funding formulas itself; its data are one input. Brookings' "Counting for Dollars" analysis adds that much of this use is indirect. Census counts feed other datasets, such as population estimates and per-capita income figures, which the funding formulas then use. These details don't contradict the general claim. The rating is Medium: one primary source plus a secondary that agrees with it, all read at excerpt level.

**Sources:** U.S. Census Bureau, "Uses of Decennial Census Programs Data in Federal Funds Distribution" (working paper / press release, June 14, 2023); Brookings Institution, "Counting for Dollars" (2009)
**Citation:** https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2023/decennial-census-federal-funds-distribution.html
