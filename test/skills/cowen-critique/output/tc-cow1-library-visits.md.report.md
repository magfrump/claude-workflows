# Cowen Critique: The Quiet Stacks

## The Argument, Decomposed

The thesis bundles five claims, and the conclusion needs all of them:

- **Sub-claim A (the observation):** In-person visits fell about 42% from 2019 to 2025, and physical circulation fell by a similar share.
- **Sub-claim B (the attribution):** The fall is driven by demand, meaning residents choosing not to come, rather than by supply, meaning the library being less available.
- **Sub-claim C (the mechanism):** The demand shift is specifically a loss of capacity for sustained attention, not a shift to other venues, formats, or media.
- **Sub-claim D (the generalization):** The library is a "canary." The same erosion will show up in jury service, consultation, and civic deliberation.
- **Sub-claim E (the prescription):** The library should rebrand as a refuge for deep attention instead of "chasing footfall."

Sub-claim B is where the argument is weakest. The draft's own evidence undercuts it.

## What Survives the Inversion

Here is the inversion, argued sincerely: *Brennholt's attention is roughly where it always was. What collapsed was access to the library.* In 2021 standard branch hours went from 60 to 36 a week, a 40% cut. Visits fell 42%. Those two numbers are nearly identical, and the draft puts them three paragraphs apart without ever comparing them.

Run the arithmetic on the draft's own figures, assuming standard hours applied roughly system-wide:

- 2019: 1.9M visits across 60 weekly hours, about 31,700 visits per weekly open hour.
- 2025: 1.1M visits across 36 weekly hours, about 30,600 visits per weekly open hour.

That is a decline of roughly 3.5% in visits per open hour. On these numbers, when the library is open, people come at almost the same rate they did before the pandemic. I'm fairly confident this is the central problem with the piece. My uncertainty is mainly about whether every branch actually moved to standard hours and how the hours varied across the year. Even with generous slack, though, the hours cut absorbs most of the decline.

Checking the draft's other evidence against the inversion:

- **"Consistency across the system"** survives the inversion better than it survives the thesis. A uniform decline across affluent and working-class areas, weekdays and Saturdays, is exactly what a system-wide supply shock predicts. A cultural shift in attention should vary by age, education, and household type. Uniformity is evidence of a policy change, not a culture change.
- **Children's rooms falling less steeply** is awkward for the thesis. If attention erosion is generational and screen-driven, children should be hit hardest. Under the hours explanation, a smaller decline is easy to explain: school-linked visits and fixed storytime slots would be less sensitive to cuts in opening hours.
- **"Programming didn't move the headline figure"** isn't a test of demand. A handful of workshops cannot offset 24 lost hours a week. The draft says attendance at them was "respectable," which is mild evidence *for* latent demand.
- **The survey, the teachers, and the bookshops** all survive the inversion, but they are suggestive, not probative. See below.

What the draft has already defused: almost nothing. It names "budgets or buildings" in its second paragraph as "tempting" and then moves on without making an argument against it.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so none of the claims here have been independently verified. The claims most worth checking, in order of how much the argument depends on them:

1. **Branch hours by year and by branch.** If the 60→36 cut was system-wide and lasted through 2025, it decides the question. If it was partial or later reversed, the attention thesis has more room.
2. **The visit series year by year (2019–2025),** especially 2020–21. COVID closures fall inside the window, and the shape of the recovery matters a great deal.
3. **The 2024 Reader Survey:** sample, wording, whether earlier waves exist, and response rate.
4. **The e-book "less than a third opened" metric:** how "opened" is measured, and whether any comparable figure exists for print. No one knows how much of a borrowed paper book gets read.
5. **The bookshop sales-mix claims:** whose data, and over what period.

## The Boring Explanation

The boring explanation is that the council cut hours by 40%, visits fell by about 40%, and a pandemic in the middle of the series broke people's habits. On the draft's own numbers that gets well past the usual 80%. It may account for nearly all of the headline drop.

A second, almost-as-boring layer covers circulation and digital. Fewer open hours mean fewer checkouts. Modest e-book growth during a budget squeeze may reflect licensing costs and holds queues rather than appetite. Publisher e-book licences for libraries are typically expensive and time-limited, though I'd want a fact-check on Brennholt's situation specifically.

What's left for the attention thesis is the survey result and the anecdotes. That residual is real and interesting, but it is far smaller than "the stacks are quiet because we have changed."

## Revealed vs. Stated

- **The survey is a stated preference.** "I find it harder to read for long stretches than I used to" is something most people would say in any decade. It's partly nostalgia, partly aging, partly the fact that adult life leaves less time than school did. Without a baseline from 2010 or 1995 it can't carry a trend. It's also a library-run survey on a topic where the institution has a narrative at stake. That doesn't mean bad faith, but it is a reason for caution.
- **Revealed long-form behavior points the other way.** People in Brennholt are presumably doing plenty of sustained-attention activities: multi-hour podcasts, audiobooks, binge-watched series, long video games, long-form fantasy and romance novels, which by many accounts have boomed among younger readers in recent years (worth verifying locally). Attention may not be vanishing. It may be moving to media the library doesn't measure.
- **The council's revealed preference.** The most informative behavior in the draft belongs to the council, not the residents. It cut hours, which reveals how it actually valued the library. The piece then asks the same council to treat the visit figures as "an early indicator of a civic problem." It was an early indicator, but of the council's own decision.
- **The equity point is more right than the draft realizes.** "Residents with money can buy quiet" is a strong observation. Followed through, it argues for restoring hours: the people hit hardest by a 24-hour weekly cut are the ones with nowhere else to go. That is a revealed-preference story about who depended on the library, and it doesn't need the attention thesis.

## The Analogy

Public transit is the structural analogy here. Transit planners know the "death spiral" well: service is cut, so ridership falls, so the lower ridership justifies further cuts. Ridership responds to *frequency and reliability*, not just to whether service exists. A bus that comes every 30 minutes doesn't get half the riders of one that comes every 15. It often gets fewer, because people stop building their routines around it.

Libraries likely work the same way. Under 60 hours, "I'll drop by after work" was a safe default. Under 36, people have to check whether the branch is open, and the habit dies. This predicts that visits per open hour will drift down slowly *after* an hours cut as habits fade. That is roughly what the 3.5% residual looks like. It's a cleaner explanation of the leftover than attention erosion.

The analogy also shows the problem with the prescription. No transit agency fixes a death spiral by rebranding the remaining buses as "refuges for deep commuting." It restores frequency.

## Contingent Assumptions

- **The library's core function is reading books.** That is contingent and somewhat dated. Modern branches also provide wifi, warmth, toilets, printing, job-search help, and children's programming. If those uses fell with hours, the visit figures aren't measuring attention at all.
- **2019 is a neutral baseline.** It is the last pre-pandemic year. Any 2019–2025 comparison runs straight through a structural break, and the draft treats the window as a smooth trend.
- **Library visits are a good proxy for civic attention.** The jump from "fewer library visits" to "a city that will make decisions by headline" assumes the library was where Brennholt practised sustained attention. Most sustained attention has always happened at home, at work, and in school.
- **"Like much of the developed world."** The draft offers no comparison cities. Did libraries that *kept* their hours lose 40% of visits? That comparison would be the strongest test available, and the claim depends on it.

## What the Market Says

The draft cites one market signal, and it fits awkwardly. Bookshops report a shift in *mix* toward short nonfiction and illustrated titles. They don't report a collapse in sales. If attention for books were collapsing, local book retail should be shrinking along with the library. A changing mix while the shops stay in business suggests demand for books persists in a different shape, possibly bought or borrowed digitally rather than visited for. If Brennholt bookshop sales are stable or growing, that is hard to square with the thesis.

## Overall Assessment

**Strong:** Sub-claim A, assuming the figures check out. The equity observation, which is the best paragraph in the piece and deserves more space. The worry behind sub-claim D is legitimate as a question, even though this evidence doesn't establish it.

**Weak:** Sub-claim B is close to refuted by the draft's own hours figure. Sub-claim C depends on an unbaselined survey and anecdotes. Sub-claim E prescribes a demand-side remedy for what looks like a supply-side problem.

**The single most important fix:** confront the hours cut directly. Either show that visits per open hour fell substantially, which would require different numbers than the ones in the draft, or reframe the piece. A version that survives scrutiny might read: *"The council cut library hours by 40% and visits fell by 40%. We are treating a policy choice as a cultural fate."* That essay is more counterintuitive, more actionable, and better supported, and it keeps the equity argument. If you want to keep the attention thesis, the evidence it needs is a comparison with libraries that didn't cut hours, plus earlier survey waves.

There's an interesting piece in here. It's just not the one the draft is currently arguing.

**Load-bearing objection:** The 60→36 hours cut almost fully explains the 42% visit drop (visits per open hour fell only ~3.5%), which would move the thesis from "cultural decline" to "policy choice," the largest possible shift. After that, in descending order: (2) the survey and e-book evidence have no baseline and can't establish a trend; (3) the refuge prescription doesn't address reduced access, while restoring hours does; (4) the canary leap to civic deliberation rests on library visits being a valid proxy for attention, which the draft never shows.
