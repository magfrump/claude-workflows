# Yglesias Critique: Fine Free Fenwick

## The Goal vs. the Mechanism

The **goal** is clear and well chosen: get more books to more residents, especially in the three lower-income branches where about one in four cardholders can't borrow. This memo is unusually disciplined. It states the goal first and judges fines against it, not against a vague sense of fairness.

The **mechanism** is "eliminate overdue fines." But the problem the memo actually measures is *blocked accounts*, and those are not the same thing. Fines are one input to a block. The other input is the replacement charge, which the memo keeps and leans on as "the mechanism that actually works."

Consider what happens after July 1. A child keeps a picture book for 31 days. It flips to lost and gets billed at replacement cost, probably $15–$30 for a children's hardcover. That is three to six times the $5 block threshold, so the card is blocked again. The parent who stopped letting her kid borrow "because they were afraid of fines" now has a bigger, scarier bill on a shorter fuse. Under the old system, overdue fines on that item would have capped at $10.

So the memo's own logic points to a sharper version of the policy. If the goal is unblocked cardholders, the thing to fix is the block, not just the fine. The memo needs to say:

- How many of the 6,400 blocked accounts are blocked *only* by overdue fines, and how many carry replacement charges.
- What happens to the block rule for replacement charges going forward.

If most blocked accounts in the low-income branches involve lost-item charges, clearing overdue fines will unblock far fewer people than the board expects.

## The Boring Lever

The most powerful **lever** here isn't the fine. It's the block threshold, plus the integrated library system's (ILS's) circulation settings. Several cheap configuration changes would do much of the work:

1. **Auto-renewal.** Automatically renew items with no holds on them, up to some limit. Many "overdue" items are just items nobody else wants yet. Auto-renew makes them stop being overdue, and it also delays the 30-day lost trigger. Most modern ILS platforms support it. It's probably a settings change, not a project.
2. **Block on items out, not dollars owed.** For example, block borrowing only when a patron has a lost item still unreturned. Or raise the dollar threshold for replacement charges, say to $25. Either option keeps leverage to get materials back without locking out a family over one book.
3. **Longer runway for children's cards.** Use 45 or 60 days before an item is declared lost, since kids' books are the ones that disappear under beds.

My guess is that auto-renew plus a revised block rule would unblock more of those one-in-four cardholders than fine elimination alone. Doing all three is better still, and the incremental cost is near zero.

## Follow the Money

The memo's accounting has one real error. The $38,000 in staff time is not cash. Staff will still be paid on July 1. Unless positions are cut or hours reduced, the library loses **$61,000 in cash revenue**, not $23,000. The staff time is freed up, which is valuable: more time for programming and reference. But it doesn't pay for the shelving.

Three more money issues:

- **The offset is one-time and the loss is recurring.** Delaying the Main branch shelving by a year covers year one. What covers year two? The board should see a recurring line. That might be a modest materials reallocation, or simply an acknowledgment that fine revenue was already declining.
- **Cash handling doesn't go away.** Replacement charges, printing, and copier fees presumably still flow through eight desks, so daily reconciliation at eight locations continues. The finance office's savings will be partial. The business manager's $38,000 should be recalculated with fines removed but other cash still in place.
- **The phrasing is confusing.** "Absorbed within the materials budget without reducing purchases, by delaying shelving" will puzzle trustees. Shelving is usually facilities or capital, not materials. Say plainly which budget line gives.

None of this sinks the proposal. Even at $61,000, it's under one percent of the budget, the memo's own figure. But a trustee who spots the $23,000 vs. $61,000 gap will distrust the rest of the memo. Fix it before June.

## Factual Foundation

Nothing was independently verified. The claims doing the most work:

- **6,400 blocked, about 14% of cardholders.** This implies roughly 45,700 cardholders. It's internal data and likely right. The decomposition by charge type matters far more than the total.
- **About one in four blocked at the three low-income branches.** This is the heart of the equity case, so it's worth showing the per-branch numbers.
- **"Systems that have gone fine free have generally reported little or no change in on-time returns."** This sounds broadly right to me, around 70% confident. The evidence I'm aware of is mostly self-reported by systems that adopted the policy, and some reported modest increases in late returns alongside more long-overdue items coming back. Cite two or three named comparable systems, since the board will ask.
- **$38,000 staff-time estimate.** This needs a methodology line, and it should be framed as reallocated time rather than savings (see above).

## The Scale Test

The library won't scale to 10 million people, but this policy will run for 20 years. The thing that breaks over time is **hold queues on popular items**. Fines mainly discipline the patron who keeps a bestseller three extra weeks while 40 people wait. Without fines, the 30-day lost trigger is the only pressure, and a month is a long time on a hot new release.

The memo's December metrics (circulation, returns, reactivated cardholders) won't capture this. Add:

- Median hold wait time on high-demand titles.
- Share of items returned more than 7 days late.

Also consider a shorter loan period, or no auto-renew, for items with holds. That targets the one place fines were doing real work.

## The Org Chart

The **org chart** question is mostly answered: the director owns it, staff execute it, and the board gets a report in December. Gaps to close:

- **ILS configuration.** Who actually changes the block rules and lost-item triggers? Is Fenwick in a consortium or shared catalog where circulation policies are set jointly? If so, the board may not have unilateral authority over some settings, and the July 1 date depends on partners.
- **Baseline data.** "Report back in December" needs a baseline captured *now*, in June, for the same metrics and the same months of the prior year. Otherwise the December report will be anecdotes, and anecdotes favor whoever tells the most vivid story.
- **Notices.** Someone needs to rewrite overdue notices so they don't still sound like bills. Fear of fines is partly a messaging problem.

## Adoption Survival

On **adoption**, this is in good shape. The policy creates a visible, concentrated constituency: reactivated families, mostly at three branches, who will notice and remember. The opponents are diffuse, mainly trustees or residents who feel fines teach responsibility. There's no obvious organized interest that loses money.

The realistic threat is a single bad anecdote, something like "the new Grisham has 200 holds and nobody's returning it." That is why the hold-queue metric matters. You want the counter-data ready before the anecdote shows up.

The memo already takes the popular framing, keeping replacement charges and "getting books back," so I wouldn't water it down further. If the board balks, the fallback is fine-free for children's cards plus amnesty for existing balances. That captures most of the equity benefit and is very hard to oppose in public.

## The Cost Disease Check

Not really a **cost disease** story. The proposal reduces spending and activity rather than pouring money into a sector with rising costs. The one Baumol-flavored caution is the staff time: savings in labor-intensive services only become real if someone decides what the freed hours buy. Name the use, such as extended children's programming at the three branches, or the benefit evaporates into general slack.

## Overall Assessment

This is a sound, well-targeted proposal. The goal is right, the cost is small, and the evidence direction is plausible. The parts that are wish-fulfillment:

- The assumption that ending fines unblocks most of the 6,400.
- The $23,000 net figure.
- An evaluation plan with no baseline and no hold-queue metric.

**The single most important revision:** pull the blocked-account breakdown by charge type, and pair fine elimination with a revised block rule for replacement charges plus auto-renewal. Then the board is approving a policy that actually reopens those cards.

**Load-bearing objection:** Replacement charges, not fines, may be what blocks many of the 6,400 accounts. If so, the mechanism misses its own goal, and fixing that changes the policy itself, not just the memo. Next in descending order: (2) the cash loss is $61,000 recurring, not $23,000 one-time; (3) there's no baseline or hold-queue metric for the December review; (4) consortium or ILS authority may affect the July 1 date.
