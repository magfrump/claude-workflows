# Cowen Critique: Vellmark's Empty Towers Are a Housing Windfall Waiting to Happen

## The Argument, Decomposed

The thesis is "conversions are a profitable windfall, so the city only needs to get out of the way." That bundles six separable claims:

- **Sub-claim A (acquisition):** Class B towers can be bought at about $95/sq ft, and that price is available for the buildings you would actually want to convert.
- **Sub-claim B (cost):** Conversion costs about $210/sq ft, and that figure captures the full cost of turning an office into housing.
- **Sub-claim C (value):** A converted tower is worth about $380/sq ft, the same as Riverside new-builds.
- **Sub-claim D (risk):** The resulting ~25% margin comes on a "low-risk" project, so it is genuinely exceptional.
- **Sub-claim E (diagnosis):** Developers aren't doing this because of permitting friction, ill-fitting standards and lack of awareness. The three steps are therefore sufficient.
- **Sub-claim F (spillovers):** Conversions revive street life, fill transit, restore the tax base and deliver housing faster than new construction.

The conclusion that no subsidy is needed requires A through E all to hold. F is the "why it matters" layer and is the most robust part of the piece. It is also the part that does not depend on the windfall being real.

## What Survives the Inversion

Here is the inversion, argued sincerely: *Vellmark's empty towers are not a windfall. They are cheap because they are hard to convert, and the market has already priced that in.*

On that view, $95/sq ft is not a bargain. It is the market's estimate of what a tower is worth once you account for deep floor plates with no light at the core, plumbing stacks in the wrong places, partial tenancies that block a full gut, and the risk of opening walls in a 1970s building. Buyers at $95 are not missing the opportunity. They are pricing it.

**What the draft has already defused:**
- The housing shortage is real enough that demand isn't the binding constraint. The 14,000-home gap and 38% rent rise support this.
- The spillover case (F) survives the inversion intact. Even a subsidised conversion program would bring residents, transit riders and tax base. That is a genuinely strong argument.

**What the draft hasn't touched:**
- *Why the opportunity is still sitting there.* If a 25% margin existed on low-risk projects, the first owners to see it would capture it, and acquisition prices would rise toward $150 or $200 until the margin was competed away. A $95 price that is stable for two years is evidence against the thesis, not for it.
- *Variation between buildings.* "A typical Class B tower" is doing a lot of work. Conversion feasibility is lumpy. Some buildings convert well, and many cannot be converted at sane cost at all.

The inversion mostly holds against sub-claims A through E and mostly fails against F. That asymmetry is the core finding.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check was provided, so none of the numbers have been verified. These figures would most benefit from checking, in order of how much the argument rests on them:

1. **The $210/sq ft conversion cost.** The draft says this comes from "gut-and-refit residential work" benchmarks. That is a generic renovation category, not an office-to-residential conversion benchmark. Conversion-specific costs (new plumbing risers, lighting deep floor plates, façade work for operable windows, seismic and fire upgrades) are typically what blow up these projects. *Confidence this is understated: fairly high, ~70%.*
2. **The $380/sq ft value.** This is a Riverside new-build comparable. Whether a converted 1970s tower in a district that "empties at 6pm" commands the same rent is precisely the question at issue.
3. **The $95/sq ft acquisition average.** Averages across distressed and lender-owned sales can be dragged down by the worst buildings. The relevant number is the price of *convertible* buildings.
4. **The 18–24 month delivery timeline.** It is plausible for good candidates, but it should be sourced.

## The Boring Explanation

The **boring** explanation is that the buildings are cheap because they are worth little in their current form *and* expensive to change. The price already reflects the conversion math. No windfall needed.

Run the draft's own numbers through a more boring cost stack:

| Item | Draft | More conservative stack |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | $95 | $95 |
| Hard costs | $210 | $210 |
| Soft costs (design, permits, legal, fees) | — | ~$40–60 |
| Financing and carry over 2 years | — | ~$20–35 |
| Lease-up and vacancy | — | ~$10–20 |
| Contingency for structural surprises | — | ~$15–30 |
| **All-in** | **$305** | **~$390–450** |

These are illustrative ranges, not verified figures. But even modest soft costs and carry flip a 25% margin to break-even or a loss against a $380 value. The interesting thesis, that the city is sitting on an unnoticed windfall, may come down to a spreadsheet that left out the lines every developer's spreadsheet includes.

**What's left after the boring explanation:** the subset of towers with shallow floor plates, good window lines and full vacancy. For those, the economics might genuinely work. A tighter version of the draft would make that the claim.

## Revealed vs. Stated

The draft's stated premise is that the opportunity is underappreciated. Look at the **revealed** behaviour of the people closest to it:

- **Lenders who took buildings back** chose to sell at low prices rather than convert themselves or hold for a conversion buyer at a higher price. Lenders are not sentimental. Their behaviour suggests they did not believe the conversion value was there.
- **The buyers at $95** bought towers in the last two years. Did any of them file conversion applications? If buyers at bargain prices are leasing them as cheap office space or holding them, that reveals what they think the best use is. This is checkable and would be very informative.
- **Owners who haven't sold** are holding dark floors and paying carry costs. That is consistent with them seeing no better option than waiting, which is also not a windfall signal.

There is one place the revealed evidence could cut the draft's way. If conversion applications *have* been filed and then stalled in permitting, that would be strong support for sub-claim E. In that case the problem really is regulatory. The draft should look for this evidence; it would be its best argument.

## The Analogy

Two analogies seem structural rather than decorative.

**Akerlof's lemons.** A used car is cheap either because it's a bargain or because it's a lemon, and the buyer can't easily tell which. Distressed office towers have the same information problem. The seller and the lender know about the asbestos, the failing lifts and the floor plate geometry. A city "marketing the opportunity" and publishing averaged numbers does little to solve this. It may even attract the least-informed buyers to the worst buildings. What would help is a building-by-building feasibility screen, which is a different policy from a developer summit.

**SoHo lofts.** The famous loft conversions worked because 19th-century manufacturing buildings had shallow floor plates, huge windows and high ceilings. Their geometry happened to suit living. Post-war office towers were designed for fluorescent-lit open floors, often with deep cores. The lesson from the conversions that worked is that *building shape*, not acquisition price, determined success. The draft never mentions floor plate depth, and it may be the single most predictive variable.

## Contingent Assumptions

Several things the draft treats as fixed are **contingent**:

- **Interest rates.** A 25% margin over a two-year build is very rate-sensitive. The economics in 2021 and in 2026 are different worlds, and the draft's number implicitly assumes a financing environment it never states.
- **Permanent office vacancy.** The draft assumes 31% vacancy is structural. If some of it is cyclical, owners are rationally waiting for recovery, which would explain why they aren't selling or converting.
- **Whole-building vacancy.** "Whole floors… have sat dark" is not the same as whole buildings sitting dark. A tower that is 60% vacant but has three anchor tenants on leases through 2030 cannot be gutted. Partial occupancy may be the binding constraint in many buildings.
- **The Riverside rent premium.** Riverside rents reflect Riverside's current amenities. A converted downtown tower is initially selling a promise of the neighbourhood the draft describes. The first converters bear the risk of being the only lit building on the block. This is a coordination problem, and coordination problems are a classic case for *some* public role, which cuts against "no subsidy needed."

## What the Market Says

This is the draft's weakest point. The **market** signal runs against it.

The thesis implies that property investors, distressed-debt buyers and lenders are all leaving a 25% margin on the table. That could be true. Markets do miss things, and regulatory friction can deter capital. But the draft has to explain *why* the market is wrong, and right now it just asserts the margin.

There is an internal tension worth noticing. If the barrier were truly just awareness, step 3 (marketing) alone would suffice. Steps 1 and 2 implicitly concede that rules are making conversions slower or costlier than the model assumes. Those costs should then appear in the model. Either regulation is blocking the margin, in which case the $305 figure is wrong today, or it isn't, in which case the policy steps are beside the point.

The draft may be *more right than it realizes* in one respect. If permitting delay and inflexible standards really are the binding constraint, then the honest argument is not "here is a windfall." It is "here is a margin the city itself is destroying." That is a sharper and more defensible thesis.

## Overall Assessment

**Strong:**
- Sub-claim F, the spillovers. Street life, transit utilisation, tax base and housing speed hold up regardless of who captures the margin.
- The diagnosis of mismatched residential standards (window ratios, balconies). It is specific, plausible and actionable.
- The framing of two problems that should cancel. It is clear and persuasive.

**Weak:**
- Sub-claims B and C. The cost excludes standard soft, financing and contingency costs, and the value comparable is optimistic. *~70% confident the margin shrinks substantially on a fuller model.*
- Sub-claim D. "Low-risk" is hard to defend for gut renovations of old towers in a depressed district. Conversions are known for structural surprises.
- Sub-claim E. Asserting that only friction stands in the way needs evidence: stalled applications, developer interviews, or a case where a conversion was attempted and abandoned.

**The single most important fix:** confront the market question directly. Either show that conversions are being attempted and blocked, which supports E and makes the policy steps the answer, or narrow the claim to the buildings whose geometry and occupancy make them real candidates. A draft that says "about a fifth of these towers can be converted profitably, here's how to find them, and here's what the city is doing to kill the margin" would be more credible and more useful than one promising a district-wide windfall.

**Load-bearing objection:** *If a 25% low-risk margin exists, why haven't the buyers at $95 and the lenders selling at $95 captured it?* This ranks first because answering it either confirms the regulatory diagnosis or dissolves the windfall entirely, and the whole "no subsidy needed" policy conclusion flips depending on the answer. Next, in descending impact: (2) the cost stack omits soft costs, financing and contingency, which may by themselves erase the margin; (3) floor plate geometry and partial occupancy mean "a typical tower" likely isn't convertible; (4) the Riverside value comparable ignores the first-mover coordination risk downtown, which may justify a modest public role after all.
