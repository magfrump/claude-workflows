## Verdict for C1: "The minimum wage increase in Seattle reduced hours worked for low-wage employees."

**Verdict:** Disputed
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

The University of Washington's Seattle Minimum Wage Study team (Jardim, Long, Plotnick, van Inwegen, Vigdor, Wething) found that hours fell. In their 2017 working paper, the increase to $13 cut hours in low-wage jobs by about 9%. Their published version (AEJ: Economic Policy) reports a drop in total hours at wages below twice the original minimum. Their later NBER paper (w25182) found that experienced low-wage workers' hours fell only modestly while their weekly earnings rose about $10. Less experienced workers' hours fell enough to cancel out their wage gains.

On the other side, UC Berkeley's Reich, Allegretto and Godoey (2017) looked at food service, an industry that employs many minimum-wage workers, and found no effect on employment, even in limited-service restaurants. EPI argued that the UW comparison group and sample were flawed, and that the hours cut, in absolute terms, was about 45 minutes a week. The Berkeley study measured employment rather than hours, so the two findings don't map onto each other exactly.

The draft's claim matches the UW findings but leaves out the opposing research. It also doesn't say which increase it means (the $11 step in 2015 or the $13 step in 2016). UW's own 2016 report found only modest effects from the first step.

Chain of reasoning: three primary studies, read at abstract level, plus a secondary critique, point in different directions, so the verdict is Disputed. The rules call for a full reading before a Disputed verdict. I tried and failed because of the DNS error. Reading the full AEJ article and Berkeley brief would show exactly which increase and which outcome each study measured.

**Sources:**
- Jardim et al., AEJ: Economic Policy (doi 10.1257/pol.20180578), primary
- Jardim et al., NBER w25182 (2018), primary
- Reich, Allegretto & Godoey, UC Berkeley IRLE (2017), primary
- EPI critique (2017), secondary
- KUOW (2017) coverage of the UW working paper, secondary

**Citation:** NBER w25182: "the less experienced half saw larger proportionate decreases in hours worked, which we estimate to have fully offset their gain in wages" (https://www.nber.org/papers/w25182.pdf)

**Sources disagree:** The University of Washington team (2017 working paper; AEJ: Economic Policy) reports that the increase to $13 cut low-wage hours by about 9%, while UC Berkeley's Reich et al. (2017) report no employment effect in food service and EPI argues the UW estimate is overstated and methodologically flawed.

## Verdict for C2: "implemented in stages starting in 2015"

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Seattle's Office of Labor Standards rate chart shows the first increase, to $11.00, took effect on April 1, 2015 for large employers. After that, rates rose on different schedules depending on employer size and whether the employer paid toward health benefits. Large employers reached $15 in 2017–2018 and small employers in 2021. From January 1, 2025, all employers pay the same rate ($20.76). The ordinance itself was passed in June 2014, so "implemented starting in 2015" is correct. Two law-firm summaries (Littler and the National Law Review) agree. The rating stays at Medium because I could only read the city's chart through a search snippet. Opening it in full would confirm the phase-in steps.

**Sources:**
- City of Seattle Office of Labor Standards, multi-year minimum wage chart, primary
- Littler (2014), secondary
- National Law Review, secondary

**Citation:** "$11.00 April 1, 2015" (https://www.seattle.gov/Documents/Departments/LaborStandards/OLS-MW-multiyearChart.pdf)

## Verdict for C3: "one of the most studied natural experiments in labor economics"

**Verdict:** Unverified
**Confidence:** Low
**Provenance:** assumed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

My searches found several separate research efforts on Seattle: the multi-year UW study paid for by the city, the Berkeley IRLE briefs from 2017 and 2018, and responses from EPI, Cato and Heritage. Cato called it a "game changer." None of these sources ranks Seattle among the "most studied" natural experiments in labor economics. They also don't compare it with standard benchmarks such as Card and Krueger's New Jersey–Pennsylvania study. I can't provide a valid citation for the ranking. A literature review or citation count comparing Seattle with other minimum-wage natural experiments would be needed to verify it.

**Sources:** Search results only; none supports the ranking.
**Citation:** None available. No source states the ranking.
