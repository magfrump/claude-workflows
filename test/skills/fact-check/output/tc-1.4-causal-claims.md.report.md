## Verdict for C1: "Austin rents dropped 15%"

**Verdict:** Mostly accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Every source agrees that Austin rents fell by double digits, but none says exactly 15%. The draft gives no measure or time window, and the figure changes a lot depending on both:
- Redfin: about 22–23% below the August 2023 peak (asking rents, buildings with 5+ units)
- Realtor.com: about 18% below the September 2022 peak ($1,659 → $1,357, February 2026)
- Multi-Housing News, citing Pew: 16.2% drop in median rent (December 2021 → January 2026)
- Pew: nominal asking rents fell only 4% from 2021 to 2025, while inflation-adjusted rents fell 19%

So 15% is too low for the peak-to-trough drop on most measures and far too high for Pew's nominal 2021–2025 figure. Reading the full Pew and Redfin reports would show which measure, if any, the 15% is closest to.

**Sources:** Redfin via Yahoo/CPA Practice Advisor (2025); Realtor.com via KEDT (2026); Pew Charitable Trusts (2026); Multi-Housing News (2026)
**Citation:** "between December 2021 and January 2026, median rates fell from $1,546 to $1,296, marking a 16.2 percent drop" ([Multi-Housing News](https://www.multihousingnews.com/austin-rent-realignment-from-fastest-growth-to-steepest-drop/))

## Verdict for C2: "because a construction boom flooded the market"

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Independent sources agree that new supply was the main cause of the decline:
- Pew's 2026 analysis is titled "Austin's Surge of New Housing Construction Drove Down Rents." It reports 120,000 units added from 2015 to 2024, a 30% increase in housing stock.
- Fannie Mae data, as reported, show almost 50,000 rental units delivered in 2023–2024, a 14% supply increase and the largest of any major metro.
- Redfin and NMHC analysts give the same explanation.

These sources also name slowing demand as a contributing factor. The draft acknowledges that in C4, so the causal framing matches the evidence. Rated Medium because the sources were read only in summary; a full read of Pew's method would show how much of the decline it attributes to supply versus demand.

**Sources:** Pew Charitable Trusts (2026); Fannie Mae data via Bloomberg/Yahoo (2025); NMHC Research Corner (2025); Texas Tribune (2025)
**Citation:** "Developers dumped almost 50,000 rental units on the city in 2023 and 2024, according to Fannie Mae data." ([Yahoo/Bloomberg](https://www.yahoo.com/news/austin-rents-fallen-nearly-two-110000847.html))

## Verdict for C3: "Between 2021 and 2023, the city permitted more new apartment units than any comparable metro"

**Verdict:** Mostly accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

**Per person**, the claim holds:
- Apartment List ranked Austin #1 among the 50 largest metros in 2022, at 18.3 units per 1,000 residents.
- Redfin ranked Austin #1 for multifamily permits per capita in Q1 2022.
- Arbor ranked Austin #1 among 60 major markets in 2023, at 8.8 multifamily permits per 1,000 residents.
- Pew gives Austin a 2021–2023 average of 957 apartments per 100,000 residents, well ahead of other Texas metros.

**In total units**, the claim does not hold. RealPage's 2022 count puts Houston (27,156), Dallas (24,147) and New York ahead of Austin (22,157). In 2023 Austin led New York by only 140 units.

So the claim is true only as a per-capita comparison, or if larger metros like Houston and Dallas don't count as "comparable." Two more notes:
- These figures are for the metro area, not the city of Austin, which is what the draft says.
- One Apartment List source gives two different numbers for Austin's 2022 rate.

**Sources:** Apartment List (2023); Redfin (2022); Arbor Realty (2024); RealPage (2023, 2024); Pew (2026)
**Citation:** "Houston returned at #2 with 27,156 units permitted... Dallas and Austin switched places at #3 and #4 with 24,147 and 22,157 units" ([RealPage](https://www.realpage.com/analytics/multifamily-permits-cycle-peak/))

## Verdict for C4: "this wave of supply hit the market just as remote-work migration slowed"

**Verdict:** Mostly accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred (migration slowed); assumed (that it was "remote-work" migration)
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Moves to Austin from elsewhere in the US did slow while the new supply was arriving:
- City of Austin: these moves peaked at about 48,000 in 2020 and fell to about 14,000 in 2024, the lowest in 15 years.
- Redfin: net gains of 13,980 people in 2024, down from 22,219 in 2023.
- Travis County: more people left than arrived in 2022–2023.

Two parts are not supported:
- **The "remote-work" label.** No source I found ties the slowdown to remote workers specifically. The city's documents describe the decline in domestic migration without naming a cause.
- **"Slowed" as a description of total growth.** Overall metro growth continued, driven by record international migration, and Austin was still the second-fastest-growing metro in 2023.

**Sources:** City of Austin demographer memo (March 2025) and budget materials; Redfin migration analysis (2025); Austin Monitor (2024)
**Citation:** "domestic migration to Austin is the lowest we've seen in the last 15 years while international migration figures are at an all-time high" ([austintexas.gov](https://www.austintexas.gov/node/94509))

## Claims Requiring Author Attention

- **C1:** No source gives exactly 15%. The decline ranges from 4% (nominal, 2021–2025) to about 23% (from peak) depending on the measure and time window, and the draft names neither.
- **C3:** True per capita, but not in total units: Houston, Dallas and New York permitted more in 2022. The data are also for the metro area, not "the city."
- **C4:** Migration within the US did slow, but no source supports calling it "remote-work" migration, and total population growth continued because of international migration.
