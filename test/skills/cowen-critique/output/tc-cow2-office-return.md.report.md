# Cowen Critique: Bringing Kestrel Back Together: The Case for Four Office Days

## The Argument, Decomposed

The memo presents one claim, "our people are asking for it." Underneath that are at least six separate sub-claims, and the recommendation needs all of them to hold:

- **Sub-claim A:** Employees value in-person interaction and feel its absence. The survey supports this reasonably well.
- **Sub-claim B:** Valuing in-person interaction means wanting a *mandate* of four days a week. The survey does not test this. No question asked about attendance policy.
- **Sub-claim C:** The decline in business performance (cross-team overruns, onboarding time, client "creative problem solving" scores) is *caused* by remote work, and more office time would reverse it.
- **Sub-claim D:** Four days is the right dose. Three is too few and five is too many.
- **Sub-claim E:** The cost is acceptable. The "small number" who leave are few, and losing them does not cost much.
- **Sub-claim F:** Leading the communications with the survey results will make the policy feel like a response to employees rather than an imposition.

A is the strongest. B is the weakest, and it is the one the memo's framing rests on. C carries the actual business case, but the memo presents it as supporting evidence rather than as the main argument.

## What Survives the Inversion

Try arguing the opposite sincerely: *Kestrel's employees have already told us what they want, and it is roughly 14 percent occupancy.*

That argument is strong, and the memo supplies the evidence for it. Riverside is new and pleasant, with two collaboration floors, a proper kitchen, and none of the crowding that made the old building unpopular. Under a fully flexible policy, people use it about 14 percent of the time. If 78 percent believed in-person work was better *for them*, and 69 percent missed the full-office energy, you would expect occupancy well above 14 percent once attendance is free and the building is good.

What survives the inversion is a different and better argument than the one in the memo. Employees may want a full office but will not show up to an empty one. That is a **coordination failure**, not a preference for remote work. "I'd go in if everyone else did" is consistent with both the survey and the occupancy number. It is also a legitimate reason for leadership to set a policy, because individuals cannot solve a coordination problem alone.

So the memo is **more right than it realizes**, but for the wrong stated reason. "We are giving them what they told us they want" does not survive. "We are solving a coordination problem they can't solve individually" does. These two framings also lead to different policies (see Sub-claim D below).

What does not survive at all is Sub-claim B. Agreeing that "in-person collaboration produces better work" is a belief about productivity. It is not consent to commute four days a week. Many people believe exercise is good for them and still don't want a mandated 6 a.m. gym session.

Push the logic to its extreme and this becomes clear. If the survey shows people want togetherness, why not five days? The memo plainly would not want to defend five days, so it already accepts that stated enthusiasm for in-person work has a ceiling well short of full attendance. The memo has not located that ceiling. The survey cannot locate it.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so none of the following has been verified. These are the claims that would most benefit from checking, in rough order of importance:

1. **14 percent average desk occupancy at Riverside.** This is the most consequential number in the memo, and it works *against* the thesis. Check how it is measured (badge swipes, desk sensors, or bookings) and whether it is averaged over all five days or only peak days.
2. **Survey response rate and composition.** The response rate was 71 percent of 640. Who were the 29 percent who didn't respond? If non-response correlates with being remote-heavy, disengaged, or already job-hunting, the headline percentages overstate support.
3. **Exact survey wording and response scale.** "Agreed or strongly agreed" and the precise question stems matter a great deal for Sub-claim B.
4. **Cross-team overrun (23% vs 11%) and onboarding (14 vs 10 weeks).** Check whether the comparison controls for project mix, team size, and hiring volume. Also check whether the tracker itself changed since 2019.
5. **The client feedback trend on "creative problem solving."** Check the magnitude, the sample size, and whether the timing matches the remote shift or something else, such as client mix or senior departures.

## The Boring Explanation

Before attributing slower projects and slower onboarding to remote work, try the boring candidates:

- **Composition.** Kestrel has presumably hired many people since 2021. A firm with more junior staff has slower onboarding and messier cross-team work regardless of where anyone sits. "New analysts take fourteen weeks" may partly mean "new analysts are now drawn from a different, wider, possibly less experienced pool."
- **Scale and complexity.** Did headcount or the client portfolio grow? Cross-team coordination costs rise faster than headcount. The 2019 firm may simply have been smaller.
- **Measurement drift.** Planning practices change. If project plans have become more optimistic, overruns rise with no change in actual speed.
- **Market conditions.** Client scores on "creative problem solving" move with client budgets, the kind of work being bought, and which senior people are still around. They are not a clean measure of office culture.

How much do these explain? I don't know, and the memo doesn't either. That is the problem. I'd guess, perhaps 50/50, that remote work explains a real share of the onboarding and mentoring gap, because that is where the research on hybrid work points most consistently. Junior staff lose the most from distance. I'm much less confident it explains the client score drift.

One more boring explanation, and it is uncomfortable. "The timing is also right because the facilities are ready" reads a lot like *we built an expensive office and it is empty*. That is a sunk cost. It is not a reason. Readers inside the company will notice this framing. The memo would be stronger if it either dropped that argument or addressed it honestly.

## Revealed vs. Stated

This is the heart of the critique.

- **Stated:** 78% think in-person work is better, and 69% miss the full office. **Revealed:** about 14% occupancy in a new, uncrowded building with no barriers to attendance. The revealed preference, at the individual level and under current conditions, is overwhelmingly for staying home.
- **Stated:** isolation is the most common free-text theme. **Revealed:** this is real, but it is concentrated among *newer* employees, by the memo's own account. That points to targeted fixes: onboarding cohorts, mentoring days, regular team gatherings. It does not point to a blanket four-day rule for veterans who have already built their networks.
- **Stated (by the memo):** a "small number" may leave. **Revealed (to be tested):** who leaves under a mandate is not random. The people most likely to leave are those with the best outside options, often senior, high-performing, or in-demand engineers. The memo notes, almost in passing, that engineering supported in-person collaboration "even" there. Check what engineering's *badge data* says.

A useful test before deciding: run a coordinated pilot. Designate two "anchor days" per week for each team and announce them clearly, with no mandate attached. If occupancy jumps on those days, the coordination story is right and you have learned what people actually want. If it doesn't, the survey was measuring something closer to nostalgia than demand.

## The Analogy

**Restaurants and nightlife.** Everyone says they love a buzzing restaurant, and nobody wants to be the first table in an empty dining room. A restaurant at 14 percent occupancy doesn't fill up by asking diners whether they enjoy lively atmospheres. They will say yes. It fills up by solving the thin-market problem: set nights, events, and a reason to expect other people to be there. The value of attending depends on how many others attend.

This analogy says two things the memo misses:

1. The binding constraint is **predictability of who else will be there**, not the total number of days. A thick market on two known days may beat a thin, scattered market across four.
2. The memo's own reason for rejecting three days, that teams "come in on different days," is a coordination point, not a dosage point. The fix for scattered attendance is *synchronization* (shared anchor days per team or per firm), not *more days*. Four unsynchronized days could still fragment. Two synchronized ones might not.

A second, smaller analogy: **gym memberships as commitment devices.** Some people want to be bound by a rule they wouldn't follow voluntarily. If that is what's going on, it is a legitimate basis for a mandate. But it is an empirical claim you can test by asking directly: "Would you support a policy requiring X days?" The survey didn't ask.

## Contingent Assumptions

- **2019 as the natural baseline.** The memo treats pre-pandemic Kestrel as the correct state of things. But the workforce has changed. People hired since 2021 made location, housing, and childcare decisions based on a flexible policy. For them, four days is not a "return." It is a new, worse contract.
- **"Within commuting distance."** This is contingent on where people moved after 2021, and on how the 25-mile allowance threshold interacts with actual commute times. Twenty miles across a congested city can be worse than forty by rail.
- **Culture "transmitted in rooms."** Kestrel's argumentative, fast style was transmitted in rooms *by specific people*. If some of those people are among the selective attrition, the rooms won't transmit what the memo hopes.
- **Survey honesty is a renewable resource.** The memo assumes it can spend the survey results on a policy employees may not like without consequence. If employees see their answers to a general engagement survey turned into a mandate, the next survey will be more guarded. That damage is contingent on this specific choice, and it is avoidable.

## What the Market Says

The memo itself notes that three days is the most common arrangement in its sector. That is a market signal. Competitors facing the same tradeoffs have mostly converged there. Kestrel can be right to diverge, but it should explain why its situation differs. "Three-day policies fragment" is a claim that applies to everyone, and competitors have apparently judged it a cost worth bearing.

The labour market will give a verdict too. A four-day policy in a three-day sector is effectively a pay cut relative to competitors for anyone who values flexibility. Expect that to show up in recruiting yield and in who leaves. Before 1 April, it would be worth estimating the replacement cost of the people most likely to leave. With fourteen-week onboarding, each departure costs more than it used to.

## Overall Assessment

**Strong:** Sub-claim A (people feel isolated, especially newer staff), and parts of C (onboarding and mentoring, where the case for in-person time is best supported).

**Weak:** Sub-claim B (the survey does not show demand for a mandate, and occupancy shows the opposite at the individual level); Sub-claim D (the case against three days is really a case for synchronization); and Sub-claim F (leading with the survey invites a credibility backlash, because employees know how empty the building is).

**The single most important thing to address:** reframe the memo's core argument. Drop "they asked for this." Replace it with "this is a coordination problem we're positioned to solve, and here is the business evidence that solving it matters." That framing is more honest, harder to rebut, and consistent with the 14 percent figure instead of contradicted by it. It also opens better policy options: synchronized anchor days, onboarding-heavy in-person requirements for the first year, and team-level coordination. A pilot of these would generate real revealed-preference data before you commit to four days.

The underlying instinct is probably partly right. Junior staff likely do lose a lot from distance, and an empty office is a real equilibrium that individuals can't escape on their own. The memo just needs to make the argument it can actually win.

**Load-bearing objection:** The survey doesn't show demand for a four-day mandate and the 14% occupancy figure contradicts it, so the memo's central framing must move to a coordination argument. This comes first because absorbing it changes the rationale, the comms plan, and likely the policy itself. Next, in descending order: (2) synchronization, not day count, solves fragmentation, so three or fewer anchored days may beat four; (3) the business metrics haven't been separated from composition and scale effects; (4) selective attrition of high-outside-option staff may cost more than the memo assumes.
