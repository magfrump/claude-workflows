# Yglesias Critique: Eighteen and Under: Smaller Classes for Every Harrow Valley Child

## The Goal vs. the Mechanism

The **goal** is clear and worth caring about: close the reading gap between east-side third graders (38% at grade level) and west-side third graders (71%). The draft says so directly: east-side families want "the same attention a child on the west side gets."

The **mechanism** is a universal, immediate K-3 cap of 18, staffed through a seniority-first transfer process. Each feature pulls against the goal.

1. **Universal application sends much of the money where the gap isn't.** The draft never says how the 157 new positions split between east and west. If the sides are roughly comparable in size, about half of the $13.8M goes to schools where 71% of kids already read at grade level. In the research the draft cites, those students show the smallest gains from smaller classes. The east side gets half the dose for the full price.

2. **The seniority transfer rule probably moves experienced teachers away from the east side.** Under the bargaining agreement, current teachers get first pick of 157 new positions, and an experienced teacher can now move to a west-side school with a smaller class and an easier student population. If even a modest share of senior east-side teachers do that, their seats are filled from the external pool. That pool has to supply roughly 217 hires this cycle instead of the usual 60. The likely result is east-side children in smaller rooms with less experienced teachers.

   This is roughly what happened in California's 1996 statewide class-size reduction. Teacher experience and credentialing fell most in high-poverty schools, and researchers (Jepsen & Rivkin is the standard cite) found that decline offset much of the benefit for disadvantaged students. I'm fairly confident in that summary, but it's worth checking. The draft calls the transfer rule "a strength." For the stated goal, it is likely the single biggest threat.

3. **Cutting the literacy coaches removes support from the teachers who will need it most.** Coaches are often how a district helps new teachers deliver reading instruction. The district is about to hire an unusually large number of new teachers and eliminate the support structure at the same moment. "Has not produced measurable gains" may be fair, but the program's value to a cohort of novice teachers is a separate question.

If the author's real priority is east-side reading, their own logic points to a targeted, sequenced, staffing-protected version. The draft as written spreads the money universally.

## The Boring Lever

The biggest **lever** here is not class size. It is **who teaches the east-side classrooms**, and that is governed by one clause in the bargaining agreement.

Two boring changes would likely do more for east-side reading than the full universal cap:

- **Negotiate a side letter on the new positions.** Hold the transfer rights for one cycle, or attach hard-to-staff stipends (say $8–10K) to east-side K-3 positions. Paying 100 east-side teachers a $10K stipend costs about $1M. That is less than a tenth of the cap's cost and aims directly at the teacher-quality variable the research cares about.
- **Target the cap.** Spend the same $13.8M only on east-side schools. That could fund a cap near 15, which is inside the 13–17 range the cited randomized studies actually tested. Note that 18 is outside that range: a 24-to-18 reduction is smaller than the treatment the evidence supports.

A third candidate the draft doesn't examine: *which* curriculum was tried. If it wasn't a structured, phonics-based program, switching curriculum and training teachers on it costs low single-digit millions. My rough guess is that the transfer-rule fix plus targeting would deliver more east-side reading gains than the universal cap at the same cost. I'd hold that loosely, but the direction seems clear.

## Follow the Money

Trace the $13.8M:

- **Geographic split.** Possibly ~50% lands at west-side schools with small marginal benefit. This is unknown from the draft, which is itself a gap.
- **The real cost is probably larger than stated.** The draft computes 11,280 ÷ 18 ≈ 627 classrooms, but students don't pool district-wide. They sit in roughly 164 school-grade groups (41 schools × 4 grades), each averaging about 69 students, and each group needs its own whole number of classrooms. A group of 73 under a cap of 18 needs 5 rooms, not 4.06. Rounding up adds roughly half a classroom per group, or 60–90 more classrooms. That implies about 220–240 new teachers and $19–21M a year, not $13.8M. Mid-year enrollment growth under a hard cap makes it worse, since one extra student can force a new section.
- **Teacher costs rise over time.** Step-and-column increases mean $88K per teacher in year one isn't $88K in year three.
- **The funding stack is fragile.** The $3.1M in reserves runs out in year three, totaling $9.3M of one-time money for a permanent cost. The "new" state block grant has no durability guarantee. The parcel tax, which in California typically needs two-thirds approval, is unpassed. If the true cost is about $20M, the gap after year three is not $3.1M but potentially $9–10M a year.
- **Some of the money buys less than it appears to.** Money flowing to classrooms run by first-year teachers produces less reading growth per dollar than the same money spent on retaining and directing experienced ones.

So of the stated $13.8M, a fair guess is that well under half translates into the east-side reading improvement the proposal is selling.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so these claims are unverified. The ones carrying the most weight:

- **"Classes of roughly 13 to 17 outperformed peers in classes of 22 to 25."** This sounds like Tennessee STAR and is almost certainly accurate as a description of it. The draft's 18 falls outside that range, which matters.
- **Long-run effects on graduation and college enrollment.** These findings exist in the STAR follow-ups. The effect sizes are modest and somewhat contested. Worth checking the primary sources.
- **Classroom arithmetic (627 classrooms, 157 teachers).** Internally consistent at the district level, but likely understated for the per-school rounding reasons above. This is a mechanism problem, not just a fact problem.
- **Eligibility of class-size reduction under the state block grant**, and whether that grant is recurring. The funding plan depends on this.
- **Facilities' ability to lease and install 22 portables over one summer**, including permitting and utility hookups. This sounds optimistic, and I'm only about 50% confident it's feasible.
- **California CSR experience.** My characterization above should be verified before it goes into the board deck.

## The Scale Test

This proposal has effectively been run at **scale** already: California did it statewide in 1996. The lesson was that the scarce input isn't money. It is qualified teachers and physical rooms, and both bind hardest at high-poverty schools.

At Harrow Valley's scale, the same constraints appear in miniature:

- **Hiring.** Hiring 217 or more teachers in one season, about 3.6 times the normal load, in a regional market where neighboring districts are also competing, will dig deep into the applicant pool.
- **Rooms.** Converting a computer lab and a library annex at every school gives about 82 rooms. With 22 portables, that's about 104 rooms against a need of 157 or more (likely 220+). The arithmetic doesn't close on the draft's own numbers.
- **Program loss.** The rooms being converted were doing something. Losing library space and computer labs is a real cost to the same children.
- **Time horizon.** Over 20 years, a hard cap becomes a permanent structural cost that grows with teacher salaries. It also becomes the first thing cut in a downturn, usually by layoffs in reverse seniority. That again hits the newer teachers concentrated at east-side schools.

## The Org Chart

The draft's **org chart** consists of "Human Resources" and "the facilities office." Some questions it leaves open:

- **HR capacity.** Who in HR runs a hiring cycle nearly four times its normal size, and with what added capacity? Is there budget for recruiters?
- **Room assignments.** Who decides, school by school, which rooms convert and what programs get displaced? Principals, or facilities?
- **Union negotiations.** Who is negotiating with the union? The transfer-rights issue needs a named owner and a timeline before February, not after.
- **Supporting new teachers.** With the coaches gone, who onboards and coaches about 150 new teachers?
- **Exceptions.** Who owns the cap's exception process? "No exceptions" sounds clean until a school has 73 kindergartners and no fifth room.
- **Continuity.** Who sustains this after the trustees championing it rotate off, particularly at the year-three cliff?

Writing the first three lines of the program director's job description is currently impossible. That is the sign the plan is still at the wish stage on implementation.

## Adoption Survival

On **adoption**, the draft's political reasoning is actually its smartest section. A universal benefit builds a broad constituency, west-side voters decide the parcel tax, and a targeted program could split the board. That is a real, Yglesias-ish insight, and the author deserves credit for it.

The problem is that it trades away the goal to buy the coalition, when a design exists that gets most of both. The **popular version** keeps a universal floor and steeply weights the dose:

- **East side:** cap at 15–16, plus hard-to-staff stipends.
- **West side:** cap at 20–21, a visible improvement every family can feel.
- **Rollout:** phased over two years, starting with K-1, which eases the hiring and room crunch.

Every family still gets smaller classes, so the coalition for the parcel tax survives. The east side gets a dose inside the evidence range, the hiring surge is cut roughly in half, and the transfer incentive weakens because the new west-side positions are fewer.

One more survival risk: the year-three review is also when the reserve draw ends and the parcel tax must pass. If reading results then look mediocre, which is plausible given teacher-quality dilution, the program faces its funding cliff and its evidence test at the same moment. Phasing helps here too.

## The Cost Disease Check

Education is the textbook **cost disease** sector, and class-size reduction is cost disease by design: it buys more labor per unit of output. That isn't disqualifying. If small classes work, paying for more teachers is the honest price. But it means the draft must show the extra labor is *productive* labor.

The two biggest absorbers here are:

- **Lower average teacher effectiveness** from rushed hiring.
- **Displaced instructional space and coaching.**

Rough guess: of $14–20M a year, perhaps $5–7M turns into measurable east-side reading improvement under the current design. A targeted, staffing-protected version could plausibly roughly double that conversion rate.

## Overall Assessment

**Sound:**
- The diagnosis of the gap.
- The core evidence that early, smaller classes help low-income kids.
- The instinct that universal programs are easier to keep funded.
- The honesty about using reserves as a bridge.

**Wish-fulfillment:**
- Treating the seniority transfer rule as a strength.
- A classroom count that ignores per-school rounding.
- A facilities plan that doesn't produce enough rooms.
- One summer to hire 217 teachers.
- A permanent cost funded by a tax that hasn't passed.
- A 12-point gain from a reduction smaller than the one the research tested.

**Single most important revision:** Redesign around east-side teacher quality and dose. Weight the cap (about 15 east, about 20 west), phase it over two years, and negotiate the transfer and stipend terms before posting positions. This keeps the coalition the author rightly wants while making it much more likely the 38% actually moves.

**Load-bearing objection:** The seniority-first transfer rule combined with a universal one-summer hiring surge will likely move experienced teachers off the east side and replace them with novices, recreating California's CSR failure. This objection ranks first because it can turn the program against its own goal no matter how well everything else goes. Next in decision impact:
- (2) The per-school rounding and room arithmetic understate cost by roughly 40–50% and leave rooms short.
- (3) The year-three funding cliff stacks one-time reserves on an unpassed parcel tax.
- (4) The cap of 18 sits outside the 13–17 range the cited evidence actually tested.
