# The Quiet Stacks: What Empty Libraries Say About Brennholt's Attention

Walk into the Ashgrove branch of the Brennholt Public Library on a weekday afternoon and you will find something that would have been unthinkable a decade ago: silence that is not studious but simply empty. The system's own figures tell the story. In 2019 the library's eleven branches recorded 1.9 million in-person visits. In 2025 they recorded 1.1 million, a fall of roughly 42 percent in six years. Circulation of physical books fell by a similar proportion over the same period.

It is tempting to treat this as a story about budgets or buildings. I want to argue that it is something deeper: a measurable sign that Brennholt, like much of the developed world, is losing its capacity for sustained attention, and that the library is simply the first civic institution to register the loss.

## An institution built for long attention

The public library is a strange institution when you think about it. It offers nothing that rewards a glance. Its core product, the book, asks for hours. Its reading rooms ask you to sit still. Its whole design assumes a patron who can tolerate a slow payoff. For most of the twentieth century that assumption held so reliably that nobody thought to question it.

That patron is disappearing. The Brennholt Reader Survey, run by the library in 2024, found that 61 percent of residents under 35 said they "find it harder to read for long stretches than they used to." Teachers at Ashgrove Secondary describe students who cannot get through a chapter without checking a phone. Local bookshops report that short nonfiction and illustrated titles now outsell long novels. The symptoms are everywhere once you look.

## The pattern across the system

What makes the Brennholt data persuasive is its consistency. The decline did not hit one neighbourhood or one demographic. Affluent Hillmere lost visits at roughly the same rate as working-class Dockside. Weekday visits fell; so did Saturday visits. The children's rooms, long the most reliable part of the system, lost visits too, though somewhat less steeply than adult services.

Nor did the decline reverse when the library tried to adapt. After the 2021 budget settlement trimmed standard branch hours from 60 to 36 a week, the library board invested what money remained in exactly the kind of programming a distracted public is supposed to want: short-format workshops, gaming evenings, "speed book clubs" that cover a novella in forty minutes. Attendance at these events was respectable, but it did not move the headline figure. People are not coming back, even when the library meets them halfway.

The digital picture confirms the diagnosis. E-book loans grew over the period, but only modestly, and the library's analytics show that the median e-book loan is returned with less than a third of the book opened. Residents are not simply moving their reading online. They are reading less, full stop.

## Why it matters beyond the library

If the library were just one service among many, its decline would be a matter for the library board. But it is a canary. The same capacity for sustained attention underpins jury service, public consultation, reading a planning application, following a council debate. A city that cannot sit with a long text is a city that will make decisions by headline.

There is also a question of equity. Residents with money can buy quiet: a study at home, a subscription to a members' club with a reading room. The public library was the one place where that quiet was free. As attention erodes, the people who lose most are those who never had anywhere else to practise it.

## What Brennholt should do

The honest conclusion is that no amount of programming will reverse a cultural shift of this size. The library should stop chasing footfall with gimmicks and instead position itself explicitly as a refuge for deep attention: phone-free reading rooms, long-form reading challenges, partnerships with schools to rebuild reading stamina from the earliest years.

The council, for its part, should treat the visit figures as what they are: an early indicator of a civic problem, not a line item to be optimised. The stacks are quiet because we have changed. The question is whether we are willing to change back.
