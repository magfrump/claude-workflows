# What Eighteen Months of Open Plan Taught Us

*An internal retrospective for Corvane Logistics, prepared by the Workplace Strategy team*

In September of last year Corvane Logistics moved its 350 head-office staff from the old Tanner Street building, with its corridors of two- and four-person offices, into a single open floor at Harbour Point. The move was controversial. Staff surveys before the move were mixed, several senior managers argued for keeping private offices, and a handful of people told us plainly that they expected to hate it.

Eighteen months on, we have enough data to answer the question the move was meant to answer: did removing the walls make Corvane more collaborative? Our conclusion is that it did, and by a wide margin.

## What we measured

We wanted to avoid judging the move on impressions, so we agreed on metrics before the move and tracked them for six months before and twelve months after.

Our primary measure of collaboration was communication volume between colleagues, taken from the company's own systems. We looked at internal chat messages, internal email, and the number of distinct colleague pairs who communicated at least once a week.

Our secondary measures were project cycle time for cross-functional work and a quarterly pulse survey.

## What we found

The headline result is striking. In the twelve months after the move, internal chat messages per employee rose 56 percent compared with the six months before. Internal email rose 20 percent. The number of distinct colleague pairs in weekly contact rose 31 percent, meaning people were not just talking more to the same few colleagues but reaching across the organisation.

These gains were broad. Finance and operations, two teams that sat on different floors at Tanner Street and rarely spoke, now exchange roughly three times as many messages as they did. The customer team and the routing engineers, who now sit on adjacent benches, show a similar jump.

Cross-functional project cycle time fell modestly, from a median of 47 working days to 43. We are cautious about attributing all of this to the move, since we also changed our project management software in the same period, but the direction is encouraging.

We also saw heavy use of the new focus spaces. The twelve bookable phone booths and the four small "quiet rooms" at Harbour Point are booked for an average of 87 percent of core hours, and the waiting list for booth bookings regularly runs to the next day. We read this as a healthy sign: people are choosing the right space for the right task, which is exactly what an activity-based layout is meant to encourage.

## What people said

The pulse survey was more mixed than the behavioural data. Satisfaction with "my workspace" fell in the first quarter after the move and has only partly recovered. Around a third of respondents mentioned noise. Some people said they now wear headphones for most of the day.

We take these comments seriously, and we recommend some changes below. But we would caution against over-weighting them. Surveys capture how people feel about change; the communication data captures what they actually do. And what they actually do is communicate far more than they did when they sat behind doors.

## Why it worked

The logic of open plan is simple. Walls are barriers. When people can see each other, they are reminded that colleagues exist, that questions can be asked, that other teams are working on related problems. Visibility creates contact, and contact creates collaboration. The Tanner Street building, for all its comforts, hid people from each other. Harbour Point puts them in view, and the numbers show the result.

## Recommendations

1. Treat the Harbour Point layout as the template for the regional offices in Dunmore and Castleford, both due for refurbishment next year.
2. Add six more phone booths to meet demand for focus space.
3. Introduce an acoustic treatment programme for the noisiest areas near the kitchen.
4. Continue tracking communication volume as the organisation's primary measure of collaboration health.

The move to open plan was a bet that proximity would make Corvane more connected. The evidence says the bet paid off.
