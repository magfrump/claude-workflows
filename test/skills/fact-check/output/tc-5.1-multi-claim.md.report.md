## Verdict for C1: "The US spends approximately 0.4% of GDP on early childhood education"

**Verdict:** Mostly accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Secondary sources that report OECD data put US public spending on early childhood education and care at about 0.3–0.4% of GDP. EPI cites 0.33% for 2015, Quartz cites 0.3% from a later OECD study, and the AFT cites 0.4%. "Approximately 0.4%" sits at the top of that range, and the more recent OECD-based figures lean toward 0.3%. The draft also doesn't give a year. I couldn't open the PF3.1 spreadsheet in the OECD Family Database, which is the primary source and would pin down the current US figure.

**Sources:** EPI (citing OECD, 2015 data); Quartz (citing OECD); AFT; Senate JEC Democrats fact sheet (2021, 2017 data: US third from the bottom of 33)
**Citation:** "0.3% of its GDP on child care and early childhood education, compared to the OECD member nation average of 0.7%" (Quartz)

## Verdict for C2: "compared to an OECD average of 0.8%"

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

The OECD Family Database (PF3.1) puts the average at "just over 0.8%" of GDP, using 2019 or the latest available data. A separate OECD publication gives 0.8% for 2019, with a range from 0.2% (Türkiye) to 1.8% (Iceland). Two caveats: *Education at a Glance 2024* gives 0.9%, which may reflect a different scope or year, and older analyses give 0.7–0.74%. Confidence is capped at Medium because only excerpts were read, though the excerpt itself states the figure.

**Sources:** OECD Family Database PF3.1 (2019 data); OECD, *Reducing Inequalities by Investing in ECEC*; OECD *Education at a Glance 2024*
**Citation:** "OECD countries spend on average just over 0.8% of GDP on early childhood education and care"

## Verdict for C3: "France banned homeschooling in 2021"

**Verdict:** Inaccurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Law no. 2021-1109 of 24 August 2021 ("reinforcing respect for the principles of the Republic") **restricted** homeschooling rather than banning it. Families used to need only to file a declaration. Now they need prior authorization, granted on a limited list of grounds: health, intensive sport or arts training, itinerancy or distance from a school, or a "particular situation" of the child. The new regime started in the 2022 school year, and families already homeschooling got automatic authorization through 2023–24. Homeschooling is still legal with authorization. Confirming this against the full law text would lift confidence; the page fetch failed.

**Sources:** vie-publique.fr (summary of law 2021-1109); education.gouv.fr (bill measures); Connexion France; UN CESCR concern noted in 2023 (via Aleteia)
**Citation:** "l'existence d'une situation particulière propre à l'enfant" (vie-publique.fr, one of the grounds for authorization listed in the law)

## Verdict for C4: "The 2020 Census counted 331.4 million Americans"

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

The Census Bureau's official resident population for April 1, 2020 (the 50 states plus D.C.) is 331,449,281, which rounds to 331.4 million. It was released on April 26, 2021. The apportionment population is a different figure (331,108,434), as is the total including Puerto Rico (334,735,155). Census Bureau pages only count as one primary source, so a second independent source would be needed for High.

**Sources:** US Census Bureau press release, April 26, 2021; Census Bureau working paper (2021)
**Citation:** https://census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2021/2020-census-apportionment-results.html#:~:text=331%2C449%2C281

## Verdict for C5: "Austin rents dropped 15%"

**Verdict:** Mostly accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Several sources agree Austin rents fell substantially, so the direction is right. But the size of the drop depends heavily on the measure and time period, and none of the sources I found gives 15%:
- Redfin, via Bloomberg: −22% from the August 2023 peak
- Pew: −4% in nominal asking rents from 2021 to 2025, −19% after inflation, and −7% in large apartment buildings from 2023 to 2024
- Zillow, via the Texas Tribune: 19 straight months of decline

The draft gives no source, measure or time frame for its figure.

**Sources:** Bloomberg/Redfin (2025); Pew Charitable Trusts (March 2026); Texas Tribune/Zillow (January 2025)
**Citation:** "22% off the peak reached in August 2023" (Bloomberg, citing Redfin)

## Verdict for C6: "[Austin rents dropped] due to a construction boom"

**Verdict:** Accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Several sources agree, and attribute the decline mainly to new supply:
- Pew found Austin added 120,000 units (+30%) from 2015 to 2024 and concluded the construction surge pushed rents down.
- Fannie Mae data, cited by Bloomberg, show almost 50,000 rental units added in 2023–24, a 14% increase in supply and the largest percentage gain of any major metro.
- Experts quoted by the Texas Tribune and KUT give the same explanation.

Reasoning: there was an observed large rise in supply, it coincided with falling rents, and analysts attribute the fall to the supply. I didn't check how much other factors, such as demand shifts, contributed.

**Sources:** Pew Charitable Trusts (2026); Bloomberg/Fannie Mae (2025); Texas Tribune (2025); KUT (2024)
**Citation:** "Austin's Surge of New Housing Construction Drove Down Rents" (Pew headline, March 18, 2026)

## Verdict for C7: "Nearly 70% of parents spend a fifth of their income on childcare"

**Verdict:** Mostly accurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

The figure matches Care.com's 2023 Cost of Care survey of about 3,000 parents, which found that 67% spend 20% or more of household income on childcare. "Nearly 70%" is a fair rounding. However:
- This is a Care.com survey of parents who pay for care, not a nationally representative statistic covering all parents.
- The same series found 51% in 2022 and 31% in 2019, so the result swings a lot from year to year.

The draft states the figure as if it applied to all parents and gives no source or year. I didn't search for Care.com surveys after 2023.

**Sources:** Care.com Cost of Care Report 2023, via CNBC (August 7, 2023) and BenefitsPRO (August 8, 2023); earlier Care.com surveys via First Five Years Fund and Bloomberg (2022)
**Citation:** "two-thirds of parents surveyed spend 20% or more of their annual household income on child care"

## Verdict for C8: "Oregon's SB 458 requires sellers to share appreciation with tenants"

**Verdict:** Inaccurate
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** observed
**Scrutiny:** abstract

Oregon SB 458 (2021) is the **middle housing land division** bill. It lets lots where middle housing is allowed (under HB 2001, 2019) be divided through an expedited process, so each unit can sit on its own lot and be sold separately. The bill text, DLCD guidance and city FAQs I found contain nothing about sellers sharing appreciation with tenants. A separate search found no Oregon law with such a requirement. Related bills covered tenants' right of first refusal (HB 3263, 2021) and sales in manufactured-home parks (HB 2580, 2021). The full enrolled text couldn't be opened, so confidence is capped at Medium.

**Sources:** Oregon Legislature SB 458 enrolled text and staff measure summary (2021); DLCD guidance on SB 458 (2022); Oregon City SB 458 FAQ
**Citation:** "any lot or parcel that allows middle housing under ORS 197.758 (2) or (3) qualifies for a middle housing land division"

## Verdict for C9: "The minimum wage increase in Seattle reduced hours for low-wage workers"

**Verdict:** Disputed
**Confidence:** Medium
**Provenance:** inferred
**Scrutiny:** abstract

The University of Washington team (Jardim et al.) found that hours fell after the increase to $13. Their 2017 NBER working paper estimated about −9% in hours and +3% in hourly wages for low-wage jobs. The peer-reviewed version (*AEJ: Economic Policy*, 2022) also found total hours declined, with no change in the chance of staying employed. UC Berkeley's 2017 study (Reich et al.) looked only at restaurant workers and found no negative employment effects. Critics of each study point to different weaknesses. Berkeley's covers only one industry. UW's leaves out multi-site employers. I saw the Berkeley study only through news coverage. Naming the specific increase and study would make the claim checkable more precisely.

**Sources:** Jardim et al., *AEJ: Economic Policy* 14(2), 2022; NBER Working Paper 23532 (2017); Berkeley IRLE study as reported by CNN Money, PBS and Nation's Restaurant News (2017)
**Citation:** "aggregate employment at wages less than twice the original minimum—measured by total hours worked—declined" (Jardim et al., AEJ abstract)
**Sources disagree:** The University of Washington team (Jardim et al., NBER 2017 / AEJ 2022) reports significant hour reductions for low-wage workers, while UC Berkeley's Reich et al. (2017) reports no negative employment effects among Seattle restaurant workers.

## Claims Requiring Author Attention

- **C1** — The US figure is closer to 0.3% in recent OECD-based data. Give the year and source, or use "0.3–0.4%."
- **C3** — France didn't ban homeschooling. The 2021 law (no. 2021-1109) replaced a simple declaration with prior authorization on limited grounds.
- **C5** — No source found gives 15%. Reported declines range from −4% (nominal, Pew) to −22% (from the peak, Redfin), depending on measure and period. The figure needs a source and a time frame.
- **C7** — 67% comes from Care.com's 2023 survey of parents who pay for care. It isn't a figure for all parents, and the draft gives no source or year.
- **C8** — SB 458 is about middle housing land divisions. It doesn't require sellers to share appreciation with tenants.
- **C9** — The evidence is contested (UW vs. Berkeley). The draft states as settled a claim that rests mainly on the UW studies.
