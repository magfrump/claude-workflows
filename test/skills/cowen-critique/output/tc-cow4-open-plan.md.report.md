# Cowen Critique: What Eighteen Months of Open Plan Taught Us

## The Argument, Decomposed

The thesis is "removing the walls made Corvane more collaborative, by a wide margin." It bundles five separate claims:

- **Sub-claim A:** Electronic communication volume and breadth rose after the move. The draft has company data for this.
- **Sub-claim B:** The move caused the rise, rather than something else that changed in the same window.
- **Sub-claim C:** Electronic communication volume is a valid measure of collaboration.
- **Sub-claim D:** More collaboration improved actual work. The only evidence offered is cycle time falling from 47 to 43 days.
- **Sub-claim E:** The specific ingredient that worked was removing walls, so the layout should be copied to Dunmore and Castleford.

The conclusion needs all five. The draft spends almost all its effort on A, which is the least contested. **C is the weakest**, and the argument leans on it hardest.

## What Survives the Inversion

Try the inversion seriously: *"Open plan made Corvane less collaborative, and the data shows it."*

Here is the case. When people sit in view of everyone, they guard their attention. They put on headphones (the draft says so). They stop turning around to talk, because a conversation is now a broadcast to thirty bystanders. So they move the conversation into chat. You would then see:

- chat volume rising sharply (+56%);
- email rising modestly (+20%);
- more distinct pairs messaging, because a two-line message is cheap where walking to someone's office used to be the norm;
- people booking any enclosed space they can find (87% utilisation, waiting lists).

That is every number in "What we found." The inversion explains the data at least as well as the thesis. On the headline metric, the draft has no evidence that separates "more collaboration" from "the same collaboration, pushed out of the room and into Teams."

I'm fairly confident (~75%) that published research makes this concern concrete. Bernstein and Turban's 2018 study of two companies moving to open plan found face-to-face interaction fell sharply, by roughly two-thirds, while electronic messaging rose. Please verify the details before citing it. The broader point holds without the citation: electronic message counts were never a neutral proxy here.

**What survives the inversion:**

- The finance–operations result has a plausible mechanism beyond channel-shifting. They went from different floors to one floor. Even so, a threefold message increase could still be substitution for hallway conversations they never had before.
- The cycle-time improvement is modest and confounded. It points the right way.

**What doesn't survive:** the claim that the behavioural data is the hard evidence and the survey is soft feelings. The inversion suggests the behavioural data may be measuring the *cost* of the change.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so none of the figures have been independently checked. The claims that would most benefit from verification:

1. **The before/after window.** Six months before is compared with twelve months after. Are the figures per-employee-per-month, and do the windows cover comparable seasons? Logistics work is seasonal.
2. **Headcount and attendance.** Was it 350 staff throughout? How many were physically in the office on a given day before and after?
3. **The software change.** When was the new project management tool introduced, and does it generate chat or email notifications that count toward the totals?
4. **The 87% booth utilisation figure.** Is that bookings or actual occupancy? Booked-but-empty rooms are common when space is scarce.
5. **Any external research cited** to support the open-plan logic in "Why it worked." The draft currently cites none.

## The Boring Explanation

Several mundane accounts compete with "walls are barriers," and together they may explain most of the headline:

1. **Chat grows anyway.** Internal chat volume has been rising across most organisations for years as Slack and Teams norms deepen. A 56% rise over roughly 18 months needs a baseline trend before it can be attributed to anything. A comparison group would help: regional offices that didn't move, or Corvane's own pre-move trend line.
2. **The new software.** The draft honestly flags this for cycle time but not for message volume. New PM tools often produce a wave of notifications, @-mentions, and "can you look at this ticket" messages.
3. **Hybrid attendance.** This is the boring explanation I'd check first. If people who dislike the new floor come in less often, they communicate more electronically *because they're at home*. Badge-in data would settle it quickly.
4. **Novelty and reorganisation.** Moving buildings forces a burst of coordination: where things are, who sits where, new processes. Some of the twelve-month lift may be that burst decaying slowly.

What the thesis genuinely adds beyond these is probably the finance/operations and customer/routing pairs, where specific teams were physically brought together. That's real, but it's an argument for **co-location**, not for **removing walls**. Those are different interventions.

## Revealed vs. Stated

The draft makes a revealed-preference argument: discount what people say in the survey and trust what they do in the data. That's a good instinct. Applied consistently, though, it cuts the other way.

What staff are revealed to want:

- **Headphones for most of the day.** People are building portable walls.
- **Booths and quiet rooms booked at 87% of core hours, with a next-day waiting list.** At zero price, staff are queueing to recover the enclosed space that was removed. In economic terms, enclosed space is underpriced and in shortage. The draft reads this as "choosing the right space for the right task." The plainer reading is excess demand for the old building.
- **Possibly, staying home.** This is unmeasured, but it's the obvious place to look.

Revealed preferences the draft doesn't report but should:

- Attrition and regretted departures since the move.
- Whether senior managers who argued for offices now sit on the floor or in the booths. Where leaders choose to sit is the cleanest revealed preference in the building.
- Sick days, which open-plan offices are sometimes associated with.

The irony is sharp. Staff behaviour, not their survey responses, is the strongest evidence that the layout is not what people want for focused work. Recommendation 2 (six more booths) is the organisation tacitly agreeing.

## The Analogy

Consider radio traffic between aircraft and air traffic control. On a clear day with good visual contact and well-understood procedures, the frequency is quiet. In fog, or at an unfamiliar airport, radio traffic rises sharply. Nobody concludes that fog makes pilots more collaborative. More messages are a symptom that the primary channel, seeing and being seen, has degraded, so coordination is moving to a costlier one.

Structurally, the draft has done the reverse. It treats rising message volume as rising coordination *quality*, when message volume often rises because coordination has become harder. The analogy also suggests what the draft should measure: not chatter, but outcomes. On-time departures, errors, rework. In Corvane's terms: cycle time, error rates in routing and finance, and customer resolution times.

There's a second, Bastiat-flavoured version. Counting messages as collaboration is like counting window repairs as GDP. Activity is being recorded, but some of it may be repair work on a problem the change created.

## Contingent Assumptions

**The move bundled at least five changes and credits only one.** Corvane got a new building, consolidation onto one floor, removal of walls, new bookable focus spaces, and new software. The draft attributes the result to "walls are barriers," but the cleanest wins it reports (finance/ops, customer/routing) look like effects of *being on the same floor and adjacent*. Co-located teams with doors might have produced the same gain.

**Why this matters for Recommendation 1.** Is the Dunmore or Castleford problem one of teams split across floors? If those offices are already single-floor, or are smaller and more operational, the contingent factor that did the work may simply be absent. The template being exported would be the part with the weakest evidence.

**The measurement regime is contingent too.** The draft assumes communication volume is a stable measure of collaboration. Once Recommendation 4 makes it "the primary measure of collaboration health," it becomes a target, and Goodhart's law applies. Push it to the extreme: the healthiest Corvane would be one where everyone messages everyone about everything all day. That absurd endpoint shows the metric has no concept of cost, meaning interrupted attention, or of value, meaning whether the messages accomplished anything.

**Era-specific background.** A 2025–26 office exists in a hybrid-work world. Tanner Street in its last six months and Harbour Point may differ in who is physically present, which matters more than the walls.

## What the Market Says

Open plan has spread widely across firms for decades. A large part of the market's revealed reason is boring: it's much cheaper per seat. If the evidence for collaboration gains were strong and general, you'd expect firms to keep open plans while paying more for them. Instead, many retrofit booths, quiet zones, and "neighbourhoods," which is roughly what Corvane is now doing. That pattern suggests the market prices open plan as a cost saving with a focus penalty to be patched, not a collaboration engine.

It would also help the draft's credibility to state whether cost or density was part of the original rationale. If it was, readers will notice it's missing. A retrospective that finds the move "paid off by a wide margin" invites the question of who was grading their own bet.

Confidence on this section: moderate. Market signals speak weakly about any single company's layout.

## Overall Assessment

**What's genuinely good.** The draft does more right than most workplace retrospectives:

- It pre-registered metrics.
- It used behavioural data rather than vibes.
- It honestly flagged the software confound for cycle time.
- It reported the negative survey results instead of burying them.

That's a solid foundation, and it makes the analytical gap more fixable.

**Strong:** Sub-claim A, that communication rose. Partially Sub-claim E for specific co-located team pairs.

**Weak:** Sub-claim C, that electronic volume equals collaboration, and Sub-claim B, causation, given the confounds. Sub-claim D rests on a 4-day cycle-time change the authors themselves won't fully attribute.

**The single most important fix:** establish whether electronic messaging went up *instead of* face-to-face conversation or *in addition to* it. Ways to do that:

- Sample a few teams with a short interaction diary or observation study.
- Pull badge-in attendance data.
- Compare against a non-moving regional office's chat trend.
- Add one or two outcome measures, such as error or rework rates, or finance/ops reconciliation times, that a skeptic would accept as collaboration *working*.

If the answer comes back "in addition to," the draft's conclusion gets much stronger. It would also be more persuasive to the senior managers who opposed the move.

**Before rolling out to Dunmore and Castleford,** reframe Recommendation 1 around what likely worked: put interdependent teams on the same floor and next to each other, with plenty of enclosed space. The current wording is "replicate open plan."

**Load-bearing objection:** The top objection is that the rise in chat and email is likely substitution for face-to-face conversation rather than added collaboration. It ranks first because, if true, it doesn't weaken the thesis but reverses the sign of its headline evidence. Next, in descending order of decision impact: (2) the gains that look real stem from single-floor co-location, not wall removal, which changes what should be exported to the regional offices; (3) headphones and an 87% booked, waitlisted booth supply are a revealed preference for enclosed space, so the focus-space problem is larger than six booths; (4) hybrid attendance shifts and secular chat growth may account for much of the volume increase without any layout effect.
