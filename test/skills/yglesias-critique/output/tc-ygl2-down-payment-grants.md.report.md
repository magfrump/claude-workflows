# Yglesias Critique: HomeStart — Putting Ownership Back Within Reach in Port Aldine

## The Goal vs. the Mechanism

The **goal** is a good one, and I take it at face value. The committee wants Port Aldine's teachers, nurses, electricians and bus drivers to own homes in the region where they work, and to start building equity in their early thirties rather than their forties. The brief is right that homeownership is the main wealth-building vehicle for the American middle class, and right that a lost decade of equity compounds across generations.

The **mechanism** is a $30,000 down-payment grant, and it works against that goal. The best evidence for this is the brief's own "Market context" section:

- The metro permits about 3,100 homes a year, below household formation.
- Homeowner vacancy has been under 1% for six years.
- Homes at or below $600,000 get multiple offers within a week.
- First-time buyers "are losing bidding wars to buyers with more cash."

The brief then concludes: "That is exactly why a down-payment grant is the right tool. It gives our families the cash they need to compete."

That is the wrong conclusion. In a bidding war over a fixed set of homes, handing 4,000 bidders an extra $30,000 does not create more winners. It raises the winning bid. The same number of sub-$600k homes changes hands each year, and they change hands at higher prices. The subsidy capitalizes into the price, and the seller keeps it. This is the textbook result for demand subsidies in supply-constrained markets. It is the same reason economists were skeptical of the 2024 federal $25,000 down-payment proposal, and the reason many researchers believe federal student aid partly flowed into tuition. The brief has accurately described a market where this effect will be strongest.

The causal chain runs like this:

1. Grant recipients bid higher.
2. Starter-home prices in the $400–600k band rise.
3. Non-recipients in the same income band, who are the large majority of the people the brief cares about, now face *higher* prices and *larger* down payments.
4. Recipients net less than $30k of real benefit, because part of their grant is eaten by the price they helped push up.
5. The brief's own goal, broad access to ownership for middle-income locals, moves further away for everyone who didn't win the grant.

There is also a quiet irony. "It does not go to developers, it does not go to landlords." In a seller's market, the money goes to whoever is selling: existing owners, investors exiting rentals, and developers of new condos. It reaches the closing table on behalf of the buyer and leaves it in the seller's pocket.

If the author's real goal is ownership access, their own analysis of the market should lead them away from a demand subsidy and toward the supply constraint they already identified.

## The Boring Lever

The **lever** is in the brief's own data: 3,100 permits a year, below household formation. Prices are high because the region doesn't build enough homes, and specifically doesn't build enough *ownable* starter homes such as condos, townhomes and small-lot single-family houses.

Boring candidates, roughly in order of likely impact:

- **Legalize missing-middle ownership housing by right.** Townhomes, duplexes sold as condos, and small-lot subdivisions are often illegal or require discretionary approval on most residential land. Lot-split and small-lot reforms (Houston's 1998 and 2013 lot-size changes are the usual example) produce exactly the kind of starter homes this brief wants.
- **Fix condo construction-defect liability.** The brief's example purchase is a $450,000 condominium. In many U.S. states, condo construction has been suppressed for years because defect-litigation exposure makes developers build rentals instead. If that applies in Port Aldine's state, reforming it could unlock more starter ownership units than any subsidy. I'd want to check the state's law before leaning on this.
- **Eliminate parking minimums and speed approvals for small ownership projects.** These are cheap, procedural changes.
- **Use the money the Council already controls.** A metropolitan council often doesn't control zoning directly. Municipalities do. But it usually controls regional transportation and infrastructure dollars. Tying those dollars to permitting performance (housing-production targets, by-right missing-middle zoning) is a lever that costs nothing new and needs no sales tax.

Rough magnitude: HomeStart moves 4,000 buyers to the front of the line each year without adding a single home. A zoning and liability package that raised annual production from 3,100 toward, say, 5,000–6,000 homes, with a meaningful share being ownable starter units, would add more *net* ownership opportunities each year than HomeStart. It would also push prices down rather than up, and help the non-winners too. I'm uncertain about the exact production response. Zoning reforms often deliver slower and smaller effects than advocates promise. But the sign is right, and HomeStart's sign is arguably wrong.

## Follow the Money

Take the $120 million a year and follow it.

**Who pays:** a quarter-cent regional sales tax. Sales taxes are regressive. Renters below 80% AMI, who are ineligible, pay into the fund. So do renters in the 80–140% band who don't get a grant, and they also face the higher prices the grant creates.

**Who receives the money, nominally:** 4,000 first-time buyers at $30,000 each.

**Who keeps the money, in practice:**

- **Sellers capture a large share through price capitalization.** I'd guess one-third to two-thirds in a market this tight, depending on how large 4,000 grant buyers are relative to annual sub-$600k sales. If the metro sees around 15,000 such transactions a year (I don't know the real number, and the brief should report it), grant recipients would be roughly a quarter of the segment. That is plenty to move prices.
- **Inframarginal buyers get money they didn't need to buy.** Some recipients, plausibly a large share of those near 140% AMI, would have bought within a year or two anyway. For them the grant is a transfer with no ownership effect beyond timing. Nothing in the eligibility rules screens for this.
- **Administration and leakage take a cut.** Lender compliance, fraud, and repayment collection for people who move before year five all cost money. More on this below.

A rough back-of-envelope: of each $30,000 grant, perhaps $10–15k ends up as genuine additional wealth for a household that wouldn't otherwise have owned. The rest goes to sellers, inframarginal buyers, and overhead. I hold that range loosely. The point is that "every dollar shows up at the closing table" measures where the money passes through, not where it ends up.

**The $1.4 billion wealth estimate:** this works out to $70,000 of equity per household over a decade. Two problems:

1. It counts all appreciation as program-caused, including for buyers who would have bought anyway.
2. It assumes prices keep rising. That depends on supply staying constrained, which is the problem the brief says it wants to solve. If the region fixes its housing shortage, the equity projection shrinks. The program's success metric is quietly tied to the affordability crisis continuing.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so the claims below are unverified. The ones carrying the most weight:

- **"68 percent said they could afford a monthly mortgage payment."** This is self-reported and load-bearing. On a $450,000 condo with 10% down, the loan is $405,000. At roughly 6.5% over 30 years, principal and interest come to about **$2,560 a month**. Add property tax, insurance and condo HOA dues and you plausibly reach **$3,300–3,500 a month**. At a 28% front-end ratio, that needs a household income near $140–150k. Whether that sits inside 80–140% AMI depends on Port Aldine's AMI, which the brief doesn't state. My confidence that many self-reported "yes" answers would fail actual underwriting is moderate, maybe 60%. This needs checking against real lender qualification math.
- **The framing that the "10% down payment" is the barrier.** FHA loans require 3.5% down and several conventional products allow 3%. If buyers can already get in with $14–16k down, their $14,000 in savings isn't obviously the binding constraint. Competitiveness against cash and larger-down-payment offers is, and that is the capitalization problem again. I'm fairly confident these low-down-payment products exist. Whether they're competitive in Port Aldine's bidding wars is a separate, local question.
- **The 3,100 permits and under-1% vacancy figures.** If accurate, they are the strongest argument *against* the mechanism. Worth verifying because so much depends on them.
- **The 2005 affordability comparison and the $540k median.** Plausible as stated. They should be sourced.
- **The "Council's wealth-building model."** It's unpublished as far as the brief shows. Its appreciation assumption needs to be disclosed.

## The Scale Test

HomeStart doesn't need a 1000x multiplier to hit **scale** problems. At 4,000 grants a year in a market permitting 3,100 new homes, it is already large relative to the market it operates in.

What breaks:

- **Price effects concentrate right under the $600k cap.** Sellers and builders price to the threshold, and homes at $610k get nudged to $599k with fewer concessions. Since the metro median is $540k, the cap covers more than half the market. That is not a "starter home" filter.
- **Rationing becomes the real policy.** The eligible population of first-time buyers at 80–140% AMI in a seven-county metro is likely in the tens of thousands. With 4,000 slots, the grants either run out on a first-come basis, which rewards the best-connected lenders and agents, or go by lottery. Either way, the brief's "open to any qualifying household" fairness claim doesn't survive contact with the cap.
- **Neighboring jurisdictions copy it.** If adjacent metros or the state copy the program, the competitive edge disappears and only the price effect remains. Run it for 20 years and the $30k becomes a structural part of what starter homes cost, much as a mortgage-interest deduction gets priced into home values. Ending the program then means a visible price shock.
- **The five-year forgiveness creates lock-in.** Recipients who need to move for a job, a divorce or a growing family in years one through four owe money back. At scale, that's thousands of households a year deciding whether to stay in the wrong home or write a check.

## The Org Chart

"No new bureaucracy" is the brief's weakest administrative claim. Write the **org chart**:

- **Who certifies eligibility?** Lenders verify income for underwriting, but HomeStart's 80–140% AMI band has a *ceiling*, which lenders don't normally enforce. They need a household-size-adjusted AMI table, rules for gift income, and a first-time-buyer definition (the HUD standard is "no ownership in three years," for instance).
- **Who verifies primary residence for five years?** The forgiveness schedule depends on it. Someone has to detect when a recipient moves out and rents the unit, and then collect. Lenders don't do this post-closing.
- **Who collects the unforgiven balance?** This requires lien recording, payoff processing at resale or refinance, and handling defaults. That is a loan-servicing operation, whether or not it's called a bureaucracy.
- **Who runs the queue and audits for fraud?** A $120M/year program with income ceilings and a residency requirement attracts gaming, such as understated income or straw buyers. The Council needs an audit function.
- **Why would lenders participate?** It costs them compliance overhead and potential liability for eligibility errors. They'll want a fee, or they'll want the program because it increases loan volume. That itself tells you something about who benefits.
- **Who owns the program after the committee members rotate off?** Some Council staff unit with a director and a budget line.

The first three lines of the job description might read: *"Director, HomeStart Program. Manages eligibility certification, lender partnerships, a forgivable-loan portfolio of ~20,000 liens, residency compliance, and recapture collections."* That's a real job with real staff, and the brief should budget for it, likely in the low single-digit percent of program cost. The track record of state housing finance agencies running similar down-payment-assistance programs is decent but unglamorous. The brief should cite which agency's model it's borrowing.

Also confirm that the Metropolitan Council has the legal authority to levy a regional sales tax by ballot. If it requires state enabling legislation, that sits on the critical path.

## Adoption Survival

On **adoption** dynamics, HomeStart has an unusual profile:

- **Visible, concentrated winners.** 4,000 households a year who got $30k will like it.
- **Even more motivated defenders.** Realtors, mortgage lenders and home sellers benefit from price support and transaction volume. They will defend the program forcefully at every renewal. This makes it *politically durable* but not *good*. It's how the program could survive long after evidence shows it's mostly propping up prices.
- **Diffuse, invisible losers.** Non-winning renters face higher prices but won't trace the cause to HomeStart. Sales-tax payers feel a quarter-cent at the register.
- **One visible vulnerability: the lottery or queue.** Every year, thousands of eligible households will be turned away. "My neighbor got $30,000 and I paid the tax" is a durable complaint.

**The popular version** that gets most of the benefit: keep the "help local workers own homes" framing, which polls well, but point the money at supply and permanence:

- **A starter-home construction fund.** Low-cost construction financing or infrastructure grants for condo, townhome and small-lot projects sold to income-eligible first-time buyers. It adds units instead of bidding for existing ones.
- **Shared-equity or community land trust homes.** The subsidy stays with the unit and helps buyer after buyer, rather than leaving with the first seller.
- **If grants stay, pair and condition them.** Limit grants to newly built homes, or to municipalities that have adopted by-right missing-middle zoning. That way each grant is matched by added supply and the price effect is diluted. Or cut the cap well below the $540k median, so the program actually targets starter homes.

The supply-paired version probably delivers 70–80% of HomeStart's political appeal ("we're building homes our teachers can buy") with a far better price effect. The brief needs a strong reason why the pure demand subsidy justifies giving up the supply side.

## The Cost Disease Check

HomeStart itself doesn't buy construction, so **cost disease** is indirect. It is a demand subsidy in a sector where output (new homes) is fixed by regulation, which produces the same outcome cost disease does: more money, the same output, a higher price per unit.

If the committee redirects money toward construction, as recommended above, cost disease becomes directly relevant. Construction is a classic cost-diseased sector. Per-unit subsidized affordable housing in high-cost U.S. metros routinely runs $500k–$800k+ a unit, driven by prevailing-wage rules, layered financing, and process costs. A construction fund that copies the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit's complexity would hit the same problem. That's the argument for pairing any construction money with the boring regulatory levers (by-right approval, defect-liability reform, parking reform), which lower per-unit cost rather than just paying it.

## Overall Assessment

**What's sound:**

- The goal.
- The focus on wealth-building rather than only rent burden.
- The survey effort.
- Most of all, the market analysis. The "Market context" section is honest and correct about the constraint, and that honesty is the brief's best asset.
- The homebuyer education requirement and the five-year residency condition are reasonable guardrails.

**What's wish-fulfillment:**

- "It gives our families the cash they need to compete." It gives them cash to bid higher against each other.
- "No new bureaucracy." It's a loan-servicing operation.
- "Every dollar shows up at the closing table." True, and much of it walks out with the seller.
- A wealth projection that only works if the shortage continues.

**The single most important revision:** rebuild the proposal around the constraint the brief already names. Either convert HomeStart into a starter-home production program, or condition the grants on new construction and on member jurisdictions adopting pro-supply zoning. A demand subsidy is only defensible once the supply response exists to absorb it. The committee clearly understands Port Aldine's market. The proposal just needs to follow its own diagnosis.

**Load-bearing objection:** Price capitalization in a market the brief itself calls supply-locked is the top objection. If true, the grant mostly transfers money to sellers and makes ownership harder for non-recipients, so the mechanism must change rather than be tuned. Next in order of decision impact: (2) poor targeting and rationing, meaning inframarginal recipients, a $600k cap above the metro median, and a queue that turns away most eligible households; (3) the hidden administrative and recapture burden behind "no new bureaucracy," along with a regressive funding source; (4) a $1.4B wealth estimate that depends on the shortage persisting.
