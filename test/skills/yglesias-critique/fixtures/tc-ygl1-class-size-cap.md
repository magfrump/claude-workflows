# Eighteen and Under: Smaller Classes for Every Harrow Valley Child

*A proposal to the Harrow Valley Unified School District Board of Trustees*

## The gap we keep promising to close

Every spring the district publishes its reading results, and every spring the picture is the same. At our west-side elementary schools, 71 percent of third graders read at grade level. At our east-side schools, where more than 80 percent of students qualify for free or reduced-price lunch, the figure is 38 percent. We have tried new curricula, a literacy coach initiative and two rounds of extended-day programming. The gap has barely moved in a decade.

The families on the east side of Harrow Valley are not asking for miracles. They are asking that their children get the same attention a child on the west side gets. This proposal is about delivering that attention in the most direct way available to us: fewer children in each room.

## What the research tells us

The case for small classes in the early grades is one of the better-supported findings in education research. Randomized studies of kindergarten through third grade found that students placed in classes of roughly 13 to 17 outperformed peers in classes of 22 to 25, and that the gains were largest for low-income students and students of color. The effects persisted into later grades, showing up in graduation rates and college enrollment years afterward.

The intuition is simple. A first-grade teacher with 26 students spends much of the day managing the room. A teacher with 18 students can sit with a struggling reader for ten minutes without the rest of the class coming apart. For children who arrive at school without books at home, those ten minutes are the whole ballgame.

## The proposal

Beginning in August of next year, every kindergarten through third-grade classroom in all 41 Harrow Valley elementary schools will be capped at 18 students.

Today the district enrolls 11,280 students in those grades, in 470 classrooms averaging 24 students each. Meeting the cap requires about 627 classrooms, or roughly 157 additional teachers. At the district's fully loaded cost of $88,000 per teacher, that is about $13.8 million per year.

We propose to fund it through three sources:

1. Redirecting $6.2 million from the literacy coach program, which has not produced measurable gains.
2. Applying $4.5 million from the state's new early-literacy block grant, for which class-size reduction is an eligible use.
3. Drawing $3.1 million per year from district reserves for the first three years, while we pursue a local parcel tax.

Classroom space will come from converting computer labs and library annexes, supplemented by 22 portable classrooms that the facilities office estimates it can lease and install over the summer.

## Why all schools, all at once

Some trustees have asked whether we should begin with the east-side schools alone. We believe that would be a mistake. A cap that applies only to some schools invites the perception that the district is playing favorites, and it would split the board along geographic lines at exactly the moment we need unity to pass a parcel tax. A universal cap is also simpler to explain to parents and simpler to administer: one number, every school, no exceptions.

Just as importantly, fairness matters. West-side children deserve small classes too, and west-side families are the voters who will decide the parcel tax. A policy that benefits every family builds the broad coalition that keeps it funded.

## Hiring

Human Resources will open 157 new K-3 positions in February, alongside the roughly 60 vacancies the district fills in a typical year. Under our collective bargaining agreement, current district teachers have first right to transfer into any newly created position in order of seniority before positions are posted externally. We view this as a strength: it lets our most experienced teachers choose where they want to work, and it rewards loyalty to the district.

## What success looks like

Within three years, we expect the share of east-side third graders reading at grade level to rise from 38 percent to at least 50 percent. We will report results by school every year, and the board will review the program in year three before the reserve draw ends.

## Conclusion

We know what works for young children who are behind: more adult attention, early. We can give every child in Harrow Valley that attention for about three percent of our $460 million operating budget. The children on the east side of this district have waited long enough. We urge the board to adopt the eighteen-student cap this spring.
