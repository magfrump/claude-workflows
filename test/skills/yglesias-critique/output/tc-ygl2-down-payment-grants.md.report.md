# Yglesias Critique: HomeStart: Putting Ownership Back Within Reach in Port Aldine

## The Goal vs. the Mechanism

The **goal** is a good one: teachers, nurses and electricians who grew up in Port Aldine should be able to own homes there, and to start building equity in their early thirties rather than their forties. I accept that goal completely.

The **mechanism** is a $30,000 demand-side cash infusion into a market the draft describes, in its own "Market context" section, as one of the tightest in the country:

- roughly 3,100 permits a year, below household formation;
- homeowner vacancy under 1% for six years;
- multiple offers within a week on homes at or below $600,000.

The causal chain follows directly from the draft's own facts. Its diagnosis is that "our first-time buyers… are losing bidding wars to buyers with more cash." Its cure is to give 4,000 bidders per year more cash, aimed at exactly the price segment where bidding wars are fiercest. When the number of homes for sale doesn't change, extra purchasing power mostly shows up as higher bids. The draft treats the tight market as the reason the grant is needed. It is actually the reason the grant won't work as intended.

The draft's own numbers make this vivid. HomeStart funds **4,000 grants a year**, and the whole metro **permits 3,100 homes a year**, only some of which are under $600,000. The program adds more subsidized buyers each year than the region adds homes of any kind.

There is a further problem: the $600,000 cap creates a pricing notch. Homes worth $560,000 to $590,000 get bid up toward the cap, because the cap tells every seller and listing agent where the subsidized money tops out.

So the author's own values should lead somewhere else. If the goal is more middle-income homeowners, the binding constraint is the number of homes, not the number of down payments. A program that leaves the stock fixed can mostly only change who wins each bidding war, and at what price.

To be fair, the reallocation isn't worthless. If grantees win homes that would otherwise go to investors or out-of-region cash buyers, owner-occupancy rises somewhat. That is a real but much smaller benefit than "20,000 new homeowners," and the brief should claim that benefit honestly rather than the larger one.

## The Boring Lever

The boring **lever** is the reason there are only 3,100 permits a year. The draft's headline example is a $450,000 condominium, and condos are where the most boring and most powerful levers sit:

- **Construction-defect liability reform.** In many U.S. markets, condo developers face open-ended defect litigation exposure. As a result, they build rentals and maybe convert them later, or they don't build at all. I'm fairly confident this is a widespread pattern, but I'd want local data on Port Aldine's condo share of permits before leaning hard on it. Reforms that work include right-to-repair periods, shorter statutes of repose and insurance-backed warranties. Each costs the Council approximately $0.
- **By-right approval for starter-home formats.** This covers townhomes, small lots, two- to six-unit condo buildings and lot splits in residential zones.
- **Eliminating parking minimums near transit and cutting permit timelines.** This matters because carrying costs during a 12-to-18-month approval are a real component of the $540,000 median.

A rough comparison: if these changes lifted permitting from 3,100 to 4,500 homes a year, and a third of the added homes were owner-occupied units under $600,000, that is roughly 450 additional ownership units a year in perpetuity. Those units also put downward pressure on prices for every buyer, not just grantees. I suspect that beats HomeStart's net effect on ownership. The lever costs a fraction of $120 million a year, and the effect compounds where the grant's effect does not. The honest uncertainty is political: zoning fights are harder than ballot measures. But "harder to pass" is different from "less effective."

## Follow the Money

The draft claims "every dollar shows up at the closing table." True, but the closing table is where the money stops being the buyer's. Here is my best rough tracing of each $30,000 grant:

- **Capitalization into prices.** In a market with near-zero vacancy and multiple-offer sales, a large share of the grant flows through to sellers. The research on demand subsidies in supply-constrained markets supports this direction, including work on the mortgage interest deduction finding near-zero or negative ownership effects in inelastic metros. I'm moderately confident on direction and much less sure on magnitude. A plausible range is that **$10,000 to $20,000 of each grant ends up as seller gains**, partly through the notch effect near $600,000.
- **Inframarginal buyers.** Some meaningful fraction of households earning 80 to 140% of area median income would have bought within a year or two anyway. For them the grant is a windfall, or at best an acceleration. The brief counts all 20,000 as "helped become homeowners." The decision-relevant number is how many *would not otherwise have bought*. I'd guess under half, maybe well under.
- **Spillover to everyone else.** Non-grantee first-time buyers earn just over 140% of area median income or just under 80%, and they pay sales tax too. They now bid against subsidized competitors at higher prices. Some of the program's gain is their loss.
- **Who funds it.** A quarter-cent sales tax is regressive, and it is paid heavily by the renters who won't get one of the 4,000 slots. Each year, a large population of renters funds 4,000 winners, and higher prices partly offset even the winners' gains.

Net: of each $120 million, perhaps a third to a half produces *additional* ownership that wouldn't otherwise exist. The rest is transfers to sellers and to people who would have bought anyway. That is a very rough estimate, and the brief should commission a real one.

The $1.4 billion equity projection has a related problem. It works out to $70,000 per household, which is plausible only with continued appreciation. Part of that appreciation is the program's own price effect, and the projection has no counterfactual. It measures equity that grantees hold, not equity the program created.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so the factual claims were not independently verified. These claims matter most for the mechanism and would benefit most from checking:

1. **The 1,900-household survey, especially the 68% "could afford the monthly payment" figure.** This is self-reported and doing heavy lifting. A $405,000 mortgage at current rates means roughly $2,500 to $2,700 in principal and interest. Add taxes, insurance and condo HOA dues, and the total is plausibly $3,200 or more. Whether that fits households at 80% of area median income depends on local figures the brief doesn't give. If the monthly payment is actually binding for a large share of the band, the "only the down payment stops them" premise weakens and default risk rises.
2. **3,100 permits a year and the under-1% vacancy rate.** These are the brief's own evidence for supply constraint, and they are the strongest argument *against* its mechanism. Confirm them, along with the share of permits under $600,000.
3. **The 10% down payment framing.** Low-down-payment products exist (FHA at 3.5%, conventional at 3–5%). The barrier may really be PMI costs and competitiveness in bidding rather than eligibility. The brief should say which.
4. **The Council's wealth-building model.** Its appreciation assumptions and lack of a counterfactual should be disclosed.

## The Scale Test

The program is already operating at the **scale** where the problems bite: 4,000 subsidized buyers a year against 3,100 total new homes. Push it further:

- **Over 20 years**, a permanent, predictable $30,000 subsidy becomes part of how the sub-$600,000 market prices homes. Sellers and agents learn it, and "HomeStart-eligible" becomes a listing feature. The cap also faces a dilemma. If it isn't indexed, appreciation shrinks the eligible stock every year. If it is indexed, the program chases the prices it helps inflate.
- **Oversubscription.** The eligible pool of renters earning 80 to 140% of area median income across seven counties is almost certainly far larger than 4,000 a year. Distributing grants "through participating lenders at closing" implies first-come, first-served. That likely means the fund is exhausted early in the year, which rewards buyers with the savviest lenders rather than the greatest need.
- **If neighboring metros copy it**, the regional subsidy race nets to roughly zero relative advantage, while prices rise everywhere.

## The Org Chart

"No new bureaucracy" is a description of the brief's **org chart** having blank boxes, not an absence of work. Someone has to do each of these jobs:

- **Certify and audit participating lenders.** Lenders earn fees on closed loans and have every incentive to approve marginal applicants. Who checks income documentation, and who claws back improper draws from "the Council's account"?
- **Track occupancy for five years.** The forgivable-loan structure requires recording liens, monitoring primary residence status, calculating repayment on early sale or refinance, and collecting it. That is a loan-servicing operation.
- **Handle defaults and foreclosures** on subordinate liens across seven counties.
- **Compute area median income across seven counties, and ensure fair-lending compliance**, especially since the brief targets buyers of color.
- **Ration the 4,000 slots.**

The first three lines of the job description might read: "Director, HomeStart Program. Oversees $120M annual disbursement through ~50 lender partners; manages a portfolio of 20,000 subordinate forgivable liens; reports to ___." That blank is the problem.

The fix is straightforward. Most states have a housing finance agency that already runs down-payment assistance, with servicing infrastructure in place. Contract with it explicitly and budget for it. Something like 3–5% administrative cost would be honest; claiming zero is not.

## Adoption Survival

HomeStart's adoption risk runs opposite to the usual one. It will probably *survive* easily. Realtors, lenders and 4,000 visible new winners a year form a strong constituency, while the tax cost is diffuse. The danger is a durable program that mostly raises prices, which is very hard to unwind once it's capitalized. Repealing it would visibly drop prices for the cohort that just bought. That durability should make the Council more careful, not less, about getting the design right before the November ballot.

Here is the popular version that captures most of the goal:

- **Tie grants to new supply.** Make grants usable only on newly built or newly converted owner-occupied units, such as condos, townhomes and ADU condo splits. The subsidy then signals demand to builders instead of bidding up existing stock. Pair this with the defect-liability and by-right reforms so builders can actually respond.
- **Shared-equity structures**, such as community land trusts or shared-appreciation second mortgages. The Council recaptures a share of appreciation on sale, which recycles the fund and dampens speculation.
- **A smaller pilot with a lottery and a control group.** For example, 1,000 grants in year one, with outcomes measured against lottery losers. That answers the inframarginal question with data before committing permanently to a $120 million-a-year tax.

My guess is that the supply-linked version captures most of the ownership gains with much less price inflation, and that it sells just as well on the doorstep ("we're building starter homes and helping locals buy them").

## The Cost Disease Check

Housing construction has a well-documented **cost disease** problem, with construction productivity flat or declining for decades. HomeStart sidesteps it in the plain version, since it doesn't buy construction, but only by not producing homes at all. If the Council adopts the supply-linked version I'm recommending, cost disease becomes relevant. A new-construction subsidy without permitting and liability reform risks being absorbed by high per-unit costs, meaning higher builder margins and land prices rather than more units. That is another reason the regulatory levers should come first and the cash second.

## Overall Assessment

**Sound:**
- The goal.
- The diagnosis that down payments are a real barrier.
- The forgivable-loan structure, which sensibly rewards staying put.
- The sizing: $30,000 plus the typical $14,000 in savings nearly covers the $45,000 gap.

**Wish-fulfillment:**
- The claim that money "at the closing table" stays with the buyer in a multiple-offer market.
- "No new bureaucracy" for a 20,000-lien portfolio.
- Counting every grantee as a household the program created.
- The $1.4 billion equity figure without a counterfactual.

**The single most important revision:** rewrite the brief around the supply constraint it already documents. Lead with permitting and condo-liability reform, and restructure HomeStart so grants follow new owner-occupied units rather than existing ones. Pilot the grant with a lottery before asking voters for a permanent tax. The Housing Opportunity Committee clearly understands its market; the "Market context" section is the best analysis in the brief. The proposal just needs to take that section seriously.

**Load-bearing objection:** Price capitalization in a supply-constrained market is the top objection, because it threatens the program's core premise that cash converts renters into owners, and surviving it requires redesigning the mechanism itself, not just tuning parameters. Ranked beneath it: (2) the inframarginal-buyer problem, which shrinks the real impact well below 20,000 households; (3) the missing org chart for servicing and auditing 20,000 forgivable liens; and (4) the regressive sales-tax funding, under which excluded renters pay for the program and also pay higher prices.
