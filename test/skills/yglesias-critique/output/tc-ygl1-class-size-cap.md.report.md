# Yglesias Critique: Eighteen and Under: Smaller Classes for Every Harrow Valley Child

## The Goal vs. the Mechanism

The **goal** is clear and worth caring about. The draft wants to close a 33-point reading gap between east-side and west-side third graders, and it wants east-side kids to get more adult attention early. The proposal doesn't say "every child deserves small classes" until the "Why all schools" section. The opening, the success metric and the conclusion are all about the east side.

The **mechanism** is a universal cap of 18, staffed through a hiring process that gives senior teachers first pick of the new positions. As written, it could widen the gap it is meant to close.

The causal chain runs like this:

1. **The district creates about 157 new positions across all 41 schools at once.** Roughly half are on the west side, since the cap applies everywhere.
2. **Senior teachers get first right to transfer into those positions.** Experienced teachers in high-poverty schools have the most reason to move. The draft calls this "a strength" that "lets our most experienced teachers choose where they want to work." That is exactly the problem.
3. **East-side schools absorb the vacancies.** These are both the new positions and the ones senior teachers leave behind. They get filled last, from the external pool, in a year when the district is hiring about 3.6× its normal volume (157 + 60 vs. 60).
4. **East-side children end up with smaller classes led by less experienced teachers.** Meanwhile, west-side schools gain veterans.

This is not hypothetical. It is roughly what happened in California's 1996 statewide class-size reduction. My recollection of the research (Jepsen & Rivkin and others) is that the share of uncredentialed and novice teachers rose most in low-income schools, and that this ate much of the class-size benefit there. I'm fairly confident in that general finding, though I'd check the exact magnitudes.

The Tennessee STAR results the draft cites came from an experiment that did *not* reshuffle the teacher labor market. You can't import STAR's effect sizes into a design that changes who teaches the kids.

There is a second, quieter version of the same problem. Spending about half the money on schools already at 71% proficiency means the east side gets a 24→18 reduction when the same budget could buy something closer to 24→15 there. If the author's goal is the gap, the author's own logic points toward concentrating the cut.

## The Boring Lever

The highest-impact **lever** here isn't in the budget. It's the collective bargaining language.

- **Fix the assignment rule.** A side letter or MOU could exempt these new positions from seniority-first transfer. The district could also pair the cap with a meaningful east-side assignment stipend for experienced teachers, something like $8–10k. Either change probably determines more of the program's east-side impact than the difference between a cap of 18 and 20.
  - The stipend is cheap. For example, 100 east-side K-3 teachers at $8k is $0.8M, about 6% of the program.
  - Without one of these changes, I'd guess the east-side effect is cut substantially. With it, the draft has a real shot at its target.
- **Repurpose the literacy coaches instead of cutting them.** Literacy coaches are usually experienced, credentialed teachers. The draft eliminates $6.2M of coaches (perhaps 60–70 people at that loaded cost) in the same year it hires a wave of novices. Those coaches are the obvious candidates to *fill* east-side K-3 classrooms, or at least to mentor first-year teachers there. Cutting them throws away the district's best supply of qualified people at the worst moment.
- **Check the curriculum.** The draft says "we have tried new curricula" without saying which. If those curricula were balanced-literacy or three-cueing programs, a switch to structured, phonics-heavy instruction is the cheapest lever in early reading, and it compounds with smaller classes rather than competing with them. I'm uncertain what Harrow Valley uses, so treat this as a question to answer, not a claim.

## Follow the Money

I'll treat the draft's $13.8M as the headline and trace where it goes. I'll also argue the real number is higher.

**The draft undercounts teachers.** It divides total enrollment by 18 (11,280 / 18 ≈ 627 rooms). A *cap* doesn't work like an average. Classes are formed per grade, per school: 41 schools × 4 grades = 164 cohorts, each of which rounds *up*.

- **Rounding:** Each cohort wastes about half a classroom on average, which adds roughly 80 more rooms.
- **Enrollment buffer:** Under "no exceptions," a 19th child enrolling in October forces a new class. Principals will rationally build in a buffer and plan to 16–17.
- **Realistic need:** Something like 700–730 classrooms, or about 230–260 new teachers, or about $20–23M per year rather than $13.8M. This is a rough estimate, and the district's enrollment-by-grade-by-school data would settle it in an afternoon. But the draft should do that calculation before the board votes.

**Costs are missing from the budget.** Leasing and installing 22 portables isn't free, and it doesn't appear in the funding plan. Neither do furniture, materials, or the administrative load of a 3.6× hiring year.

**Not all of the money reaches the target.** Of each dollar:

- **About 45–50% goes to west-side schools.** The draft's own measurable goal is about the east side. That spending doesn't serve it, except politically, which I'll credit below.
- **Part of the east-side share is eroded by the experience drop described above.** That share buys smaller classes, but with weaker average teacher experience.

My rough guess is that a third or less of the spending translates into the east-side reading gains the draft promises. That's not because class size doesn't matter. It's because the design routes the money and the experienced labor elsewhere.

**The funding stack has a cliff.**

| Source | Amount | Durability |
|---|---|---|
| Literacy coach cut | $6.2M | Permanent, but see above |
| State early-literacy block grant | $4.5M | Durable only if the grant is continuing rather than one-time; worth confirming |
| District reserves | $3.1M/yr for 3 years | Ends in year 3 |
| Local parcel tax | Not yet passed | Required to replace reserves |

If the real cost is closer to $21M, the gap the parcel tax has to cover isn't $3.1M. It's $10M or more.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so none of these claims were independently verified. The claims doing the most work, with my confidence in each:

- **STAR effect sizes and long-run effects.** The draft's description ("13 to 17 vs. 22 to 25," larger gains for low-income and Black students, effects on later outcomes) sounds right, about 85% confident. The magnitude matters, though. Early-grade effects of roughly 0.15–0.2 standard deviations are real but modest.
  - The draft's cut (24→18) is smaller than STAR's (about 23→15).
  - Raising east-side proficiency from 38% to 50% in three years is ambitious. I'm only around 50% sure it is achievable even with perfect implementation, and less sure with the staffing dynamics above.
- **Literacy coaches "not producing measurable gains."** This claim justifies cutting $6.2M. What evaluation supports it?
- **Block grant eligibility and duration.** Is class-size reduction an eligible use? Is the grant recurring?
- **The arithmetic.** The draft's own numbers check out internally (470 × 24 = 11,280; 157 × $88k ≈ $13.8M; $13.8M is about 3% of $460M). The issue is the cap-vs-average modeling, not the multiplication.
- **Facilities capacity.** How many computer labs and library annexes actually exist across 41 schools? Even at two per school, that's about 82 rooms plus 22 portables, which falls short of 157. It falls well short of the 230+ I estimate.

## The Scale Test

The relevant **scale** isn't 10 million people. It's 41 schools at once, in one regional labor market, in one summer. Several things break:

- **The labor market.** The district needs roughly 3.6–5× its normal hiring volume from the same regional pool, competing with neighboring districts. If the new state block grant makes class-size reduction an eligible use, *other districts may be doing the same thing the same year*. That is the California 1996 dynamic in miniature: everyone shrinks classes simultaneously, demand for qualified teachers spikes, and the poorest schools end up with whoever is left.
- **Facilities.** Twenty-two portables installed in one summer means permitting, utility hookups and site work across many campuses. Converting computer labs and library annexes trades away instructional space that east-side kids, the ones without books at home, may rely on most.
- **Time.** Over a 20-year horizon, a hard "no exceptions" cap converts every enrollment fluctuation into a staffing event. That locks in a permanent structural cost that grows with wages. Enrollment declines leave half-empty rooms. Growth forces mid-year hires.

## The Org Chart

Try writing the job description. "Director of K-3 Class Size Implementation, reporting to ___, with authority over HR assignment, facilities, and enrollment balancing across 41 schools." The draft doesn't name this person, and it needs one. Right now the **org chart** has no owner for the program:

- **HR** runs a hiring year roughly four times normal size, under rules it doesn't control.
- **Facilities** promises 22 portables over the summer, but that is an estimate, not a contract.
- **Principals** must enforce a hard cap while managing mid-year enrollment.
- **The union** controls the single most important variable (who teaches where), and the draft treats that as fixed.

Who owns the year-3 outcome? Who decides when a school is at 19 in October? Who negotiates the assignment changes? A proposal without these answers is still partly at the wish stage. The fix is cheap: name an owner, and bring the union in as a co-author of the staffing plan before the vote, not after.

## Adoption Survival

This is where the draft is genuinely smart. Universality really does build a coalition. West-side families vote on parcel taxes, and "every school gets smaller classes" is a much easier campaign than "the east side gets smaller classes." That's a real point, and Yglesias-style pragmatism should take it seriously rather than wave it away.

The **adoption** risk is in the sequence:

- **The results and the funding cliff arrive together.** In year 3, the reserve draw ends and the board reviews results at the same moment.
- **Disappointing results trigger cuts.** If east-side scores rise from 38 to 43 instead of 50 (plausible, given the staffing dynamics), the parcel tax campaign runs on a missed target. The cap gets cut.
- **The cuts land on the east side.** Under last-in-first-out layoffs, those cuts fall hardest on the newest teachers, who are disproportionately on the east side.

The program could end up both failing to close the gap and leaving east-side schools with more turnover than they started with.

**The popular version** keeps the universality and buys the targeting. One option is a district-wide cap of 20–21, with east-side schools at 15–16. Another is to phase in K-1 everywhere first, then extend. Every family gets something, which preserves the parcel tax coalition. The east side gets the reduction that matches STAR's actual treatment, and the hiring volume stays closer to what HR can manage. I'd guess this captures most of the political benefit of universality and *more* of the reading-gap benefit than the current design, at similar or lower cost.

## The Cost Disease Check

K-12 education is a textbook **cost disease** sector, and class-size reduction is the most direct possible bet within it. It buys more labor per unit of output on purpose. That can be the right bet! But the draft should own it explicitly:

- **This is not a three-year program.** It is a permanent increase in headcount of 157 or more teachers, whose cost rises with salary schedules every year.
- **The cost compounds.** At 3–4% annual growth, the true $20M+ becomes $27–30M within a decade.
- **What the money buys depends on staffing.** If the district fixes assignment and supports new teachers, most of the spending buys real additional instruction. If it doesn't, a meaningful share is absorbed by lower average experience and hiring friction. You get more teachers but not proportionally more teaching.

The draft's "about three percent of our operating budget" framing understates this. It is three percent this year, permanently, and probably closer to four or five percent once the cap arithmetic is done correctly.

## Overall Assessment

**What's sound:**

- The goal.
- The core evidence that early, small classes help disadvantaged kids.
- The instinct to fund something direct rather than another initiative.
- The political logic of universality.

**What's wish-fulfillment:**

- The teacher and classroom counts, which treat a cap as an average.
- The facilities plan.
- A hiring plan that hands the most important allocation decision to seniority.
- A three-year success target that STAR-sized effects may not reach.

**The single most important revision:** the board has to control *who* teaches in the new east-side classrooms. That means:

- Exempting these positions from seniority-first transfer, or adding an east-side assignment stipend.
- Redeploying literacy coaches into east-side K-3 classrooms or mentoring roles instead of cutting them.
- Rebuilding the cost model grade-by-grade, school-by-school.

Do that, consider a tiered cap (around 20 everywhere, 15–16 on the east side), and this becomes a strong proposal. It would be one of the better uses of $15–20M a district this size could make.

**Load-bearing objection:** The seniority-first transfer rule is the objection to act on first, because it can reverse the program's effect on the very schools it targets and can only be fixed by renegotiating hiring rules, not by adjusting the budget. Next, in descending order of decision impact:

1. A cap needs far more teachers and rooms than an average, which roughly doubles the funding gap.
2. Spreading the reduction universally dilutes the east-side dose below STAR's.
3. The year-3 funding cliff coincides with a results review that the target may be too ambitious to pass.
