# Vellmark's Empty Towers Are a Housing Windfall Waiting to Happen

Downtown Vellmark has two problems that ought to cancel each other out. The first is a glut of office space: since 2020, vacancy in the central business district has climbed from 9 percent to 31 percent, and whole floors of the older towers along Crane Street and Merchant Row have sat dark for years. The second is a housing shortage: the city's own housing needs assessment says Vellmark is short roughly 14,000 homes, and median rents have risen 38 percent in five years.

The obvious answer is to turn the offices into homes. What is less widely appreciated is how profitable that would be.

## The numbers

I have spent the past three months building a simple model of what a conversion of a typical Class B tower in the district would cost and return, using public sales records, construction cost benchmarks from the regional builders' association, and current rents for comparable new-build apartments.

Acquisition is where the opportunity starts. Class B office buildings in central Vellmark have traded in the last two years at an average of about $95 per square foot, compared with more than $300 before the pandemic. Several have changed hands at even lower prices after lenders took them back.

Conversion costs are higher than for a standard renovation but well within the normal range for residential construction. Using the builders' association benchmarks for gut-and-refit residential work, I estimate $210 per square foot to strip the floors, add kitchens and bathrooms, upgrade the lifts and bring the building up to residential fire code.

That puts the all-in cost at roughly $305 per square foot. Newly built apartments in the adjacent Riverside district are currently valued at about $380 per square foot on the rent-capitalisation basis lenders use. On those figures, a converted tower is worth around 25 percent more than it cost to create, before any public subsidy.

A 25 percent margin on a large, low-risk urban residential project is exceptional. It is the kind of return that, in most property markets, would have developers queuing up.

## Why this matters for the city

The benefits go well beyond the developer's balance sheet. Every converted tower brings hundreds of residents into a district that currently empties at 6pm. Those residents support the cafés, gyms and shops that have been closing since 2020. They make the streets feel safe at night. They use the existing transit lines, which were built to carry commuters into the centre and now run half-empty.

Conversions also produce housing faster than greenfield development. The structure already exists; the utility connections already exist; the planning principle of dense use on the site is already established. A conversion can be delivered in eighteen to twenty-four months, compared with three to five years for a comparable new tower once land assembly and approvals are counted.

And there is a fiscal argument. Empty offices are being reassessed downwards, cutting the city's property tax base. Occupied residential buildings would restore it.

## What the city should do

Given the returns available, the city does not need to subsidise conversions. It needs to get out of the way. Three steps would be enough:

1. **Fast-track permitting.** Create a dedicated conversion desk in the planning department with a guaranteed 90-day decision on any complete conversion application in the central district.
2. **Relax residential standards that assume new construction.** Several rules, such as minimum window-to-floor ratios and balcony requirements, were written for new-build towers and make little sense for existing structures. Allow alternatives.
3. **Market the opportunity.** Publish the numbers. Hold a developer summit. Make it clear that Vellmark wants these projects.

## A rare alignment

It is unusual in urban policy for the private interest and the public interest to point so clearly in the same direction. Vellmark has cheap buildings, unmet housing demand and a margin that should make these projects attractive on their own. The towers are sitting there. The city should make it as easy as possible for someone to capture that margin.
