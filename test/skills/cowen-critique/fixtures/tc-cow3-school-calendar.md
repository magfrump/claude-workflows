# Leave Summer Alone: Against the Marlow County Calendar Proposal

This month the Marlow County School Board will vote on a proposal to restructure the school year. Under the plan, the current ten-and-a-half-week summer holiday would shrink to six weeks, and the days saved would be redistributed as two-week breaks in October and February. The board's consultants call this a "balanced calendar." I think it is a mistake, and I want to explain why in terms that go beyond nostalgia.

## The rhythm of a year

The long summer is not an administrative convenience. It is the natural rhythm of childhood. Every child knows the particular feeling of the last day of school in June: a stretch of time so long it seems to have no end. That feeling is not trivial. It is the only period in a young person's life when time is genuinely unstructured, when boredom has room to turn into curiosity, and when a child can spend three weeks building a treehouse or reading every book in a series because nobody has scheduled anything else.

Children need a long uninterrupted break the way fields need a fallow season. The school year asks them to sit, attend and perform for nine months. The summer is when they recover and grow in ways that do not show up on tests. Chop it into pieces and you do not get three smaller rests; you get three interruptions, none long enough to matter.

## What the evidence actually shows

Supporters of the proposal lean heavily on "summer learning loss": the finding that students, especially those from lower-income families, lose some of their reading and maths progress over the summer. The consultants' report cites a Marlow County analysis showing that the average fourth-grader's maths score drops by the equivalent of about five weeks of instruction between June and September.

I do not dispute the number. I dispute the conclusion. A five-week dip, which is recovered by October in most classrooms, is a small price for what the summer provides. And the balanced-calendar evidence is weak: the consultants' own literature review concedes that studies of balanced calendars show "small and inconsistent" achievement effects. We would be restructuring family life across the county for a gain that the people proposing it cannot clearly demonstrate.

## Families, jobs and the county

The summer is also woven into the county's economy and family life in ways the proposal ignores. The Lakeside camps, the county fair in August, the teenage summer jobs at the marinas and orchards, the long trips to see grandparents: all of these assume a long summer. Parents plan their year around it. Employers plan their seasonal hiring around it. Teachers, many of whom take summer courses to maintain their certification, plan their professional development around it.

A two-week October break helps none of these. Camps cannot run a viable season in two weeks. Teenagers cannot hold a job in a fortnight. And working parents, who already struggle to find childcare for one long break, would now have to find it three times a year.

## The deeper point

Every generation of reformers wants to make childhood more efficient. The balanced calendar is the latest version of that impulse: a tidy spreadsheet in which rest is distributed evenly across the year like a budget line. But childhood is not a spreadsheet, and a long summer is not an inefficiency to be engineered away. It is one of the few things our school system gets right precisely because it was not designed by a consultant.

The long summer has been part of growing up for as long as there have been schools. It is how children have always lived, and it is how they should continue to live.

## What the board should do instead

If the board is worried about summer learning loss, the answer is targeted, not structural. Fund free summer reading programmes at every branch library. Expand the county's summer maths camps, which reached only 400 students last year, to every child who qualifies for free lunch. Send books home in June. These measures address the actual problem without dismantling something that works for most families.

I urge the board to vote no, and to leave summer alone.
