# Yglesias Critique: Fine Free Fenwick

## The Goal vs. the Mechanism

The **goal** is clear and worth defending: get books to as many residents as possible, especially in the three lowest-income branches where about one in four cardholders is blocked. The **mechanism** is to eliminate overdue fines, keep replacement charges, and clear existing overdue balances.

This memo is unusual because the mechanism mostly does serve the goal. Fines raise little money, cost a lot to collect, and fall hardest on the people the library most wants to reach. Usually this move exposes a feel-good proposal that undercuts itself. Here the proposal is already the unglamorous fix. The question is whether it fixes the specific problem the memo describes.

That problem is **blocked accounts**, not fines as such. The memo says 6,400 cardholders owe more than $5, but it never says what they owe for. Some of that debt is likely replacement charges for lost items, which can easily run $20–30 per book and which this proposal keeps. If a large share of blocked balances in the low-income branches comes from replacement charges, the board could approve this change on July 1 and still see many of those 6,400 accounts stay blocked. The parents who fear fines would also still face a 30-day lost-item bill. That bill is bigger and scarier than 25 cents a day.

So the causal chain has a gap. Ending fines reduces blocks only in proportion to how much of the blocked debt is overdue fines. The memo needs one number: how many of the 6,400 accounts drop below $5 once overdue fines are cleared. If the answer is 5,800, the case is overwhelming. If it is 2,500, the real access fix is the blocking policy, and fine elimination is the smaller half.

## The Boring Lever

Fine elimination is itself a boring lever, which counts in its favor. Two even more boring levers sit next to it, and the memo should either adopt them or explain why not.

1. **The block threshold and what counts toward it.** The barrier to borrowing is the $5 block, not the fine. The board could exempt children's and teen cards from blocking. It could raise the threshold for replacement charges, or allow limited borrowing (say, two items) on blocked accounts. Any of these probably does more for the "parents stopped letting kids borrow" problem than fine elimination alone. This is especially true if replacement charges make up much of the blocked debt.
2. **Automatic renewal.** If the library's system supports renewing items automatically when nobody has placed a hold, many "overdue" items stop being overdue at all. This costs almost nothing and reduces both the return-rate risk and the number of items that slide into the 30-day lost-billing bucket.

My guess is that a child-card blocking exemption plus auto-renew, combined with this proposal, would reach nearly all of the access benefit. That guess depends entirely on the composition of the blocked balances, which the memo should report.

## Follow the Money

Two problems in the accounting need fixing before the board sees this.

**First, the staff savings are not cash.** The memo nets $38,000 of staff time against $61,000 in fine revenue and calls the hole $23,000. But staff time is only a cash saving if hours or positions are actually cut. Nobody is proposing to cut circulation hours. The freed time will go to other desk work, which is good, but it does not pay any bills. The real cash hole is about **$61,000 a year**. The memo funds $23,000.

**Second, the offset is one-time and the loss is recurring.** Delaying the Main branch shelving replacement by one year covers year one. In year two the fine revenue is still gone and the shelving still needs replacing. The memo needs a recurring line.

The good news is that the real number is small. $61,000 is under 1 percent of the operating budget, and it is probably smaller than it looks:

- Fines are mostly paid by people who can afford them. Blocked low-income patrons largely aren't paying.
- Clearing the blocked accounts' balances therefore costs very little real cash. That money was never coming in.
- Most of the forgone revenue is the small, voluntary payments of comfortable patrons.

Say that plainly. Name a recurring source for about $61,000, or show that cash collections net of card-processing fees and cash handling are lower than the gross figure. That turns the budget section from a weak point into a strength.

A smaller point: the memo says the money will be "absorbed within the materials budget without reducing purchases, by delaying a planned shelving replacement." Shelving is usually a facilities or capital item, not materials. If those are separate budget lines, a trustee will catch it. Clarify which budget actually takes the hit.

## Factual Foundation

No fact-check report was provided, so none of the following has been independently verified. These claims matter most to the case:

- **6,400 blocked cardholders (about 14 percent), with about one in four blocked at three branches.** Internal data, likely accurate, but the breakdown of fines versus replacement charges is missing. This is the most decision-relevant gap in the memo.
- **$61,000 collected and $38,000 in staff time.** The $38,000 is an estimate from the business manager. I'd want the method (minutes per transaction times volume times loaded wage). It also needs relabeling as non-cash, as discussed above.
- **"Library systems that have gone fine free have generally reported little or no change in on-time returns."** This sounds right. Several large systems have reported results along these lines, but evidence quality varies and most of it is before-and-after reporting, not controlled comparison. I'm roughly 75 percent confident the claim holds as stated. Citing two or three named peer systems would strengthen it a lot.
- **Long-overdue items came back after going fine free.** Plausibly true and widely reported, but often tied to one-time amnesty effects. Don't promise it as an ongoing benefit.

## The Scale Test

At the scale of the whole system, this is low-risk. Many libraries have run this experiment, and the downside is bounded by the 30-day lost-item bill. The things that could break at scale are second-order:

- **Hold queues.** If average loan length creeps up by even a few days, wait times on popular new releases grow. Patrons experience that as worse service even if on-time return rates barely move. Track hold-to-fill time, not just return rates.
- **The lost-item bucket.** With no daily fine, more items may sit until day 30 and get billed. That pushes patrons into exactly the larger debt that blocks accounts. Watch whether replacement-charge blocks rise in the low-income branches. If they do, the problem has moved rather than been solved.

## The Org Chart

The org chart is mostly clear, which is to the memo's credit. Circulation staff, the finance office, and whoever administers the library's catalog and circulation system will execute it. Three gaps remain:

- **Who configures the system?** Clearing balances, changing fine rules, turning on auto-renew if adopted, and changing notice templates are often vendor-dependent tasks. Confirm that the vendor can do this by July 1, and at what cost.
- **Who owns the metrics, and are they defined now?** A December report covers only five months, including summer, when circulation patterns are unusual. Set the success metrics in advance, with baselines:
  - unblocked and reactivated accounts by branch
  - return times
  - hold-to-fill time
  - lost-item counts

  Otherwise the December meeting becomes an argument about anecdotes.
- **Does any collection agency or city finance arrangement touch fine revenue?** If fines are booked as city revenue, the city may need to sign off, not just the board.

## Adoption Survival

Proposals like this usually fail adoption in one of two ways. Either a trustee frames it as "rewarding irresponsibility," or a weak early data point gets treated as proof of failure.

The constituency created is real but diffuse: the reactivated cardholders. It becomes defensible if the library makes them visible. Report "X children's cards reactivated at the three branches," not just aggregate circulation.

The opposition will be small. It will mostly be about principle, plus whoever loses the shelving money. The memo can blunt the principle objection by stressing what stays in place: lost and damaged items are still billed, and the memo already does this well.

The popular version is roughly the proposal itself. If the board balks, a fallback that gets about 80 percent of the access benefit is fines eliminated on children's and teen materials only, plus a blocking exemption for those cards. It is harder to argue against, and the evidence it generates makes a later full rollout easier. Keep it in the back pocket rather than in the memo.

## Overall Assessment

The proposal is sound and is already the pragmatic, boring option. The goal and mechanism mostly line up, the risk is bounded by lost-item billing, and the revenue at stake is trivial relative to the access gained. The wish-fulfillment parts are in the details:

- a budget fix that treats staff time as cash
- a recurring loss covered by a one-time offset
- an implied promise that ending fines unblocks the 6,400 accounts, which depends on data the memo doesn't show

The single most important revision is to report how many of the 6,400 blocked accounts fall below the $5 threshold once overdue fines alone are cleared. If the number falls short, pair this change with a revision of the blocking policy, starting with children's cards. Then fix the money section to show a recurring source for about $61,000, and define the December metrics now.

**Load-bearing objection:** The unknown share of blocked balances made up of replacement charges, which the proposal keeps, matters most. It determines whether the mechanism achieves the stated goal at all, and fixing it may require adding a change to the blocking policy. Next, in descending order of impact:

1. The cash hole is about $61,000 a year, not $23,000, and the offset is one-time while the loss recurs.
2. Pre-defined metrics, including hold-to-fill time and lost-item counts, are needed so the December review can't reverse a working policy on thin evidence.
3. Auto-renewal is a cheap complementary lever the memo should consider.
