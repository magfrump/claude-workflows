## Persona Critiques

### The Empiricist: What evidence actually supports this claim?

User goal: Stress-test this proposal from several independent angles before it goes to the people who will decide on it.
Current task: Apply the Empiricist lens — What evidence actually supports this claim? — to this proposal.
Success criterion: A 100-200 word critique grounded in proposal specifics, with severity and a test/mitigation.

The main metric, Sunday visits as a share of Saturday visits in the same weeks, is distorted by the thing it measures. Some Saturday regulars will move to Sunday. When Saturday falls and Sunday rises, the 60 percent ratio gets easier to hit even if total weekend use doesn't change. The pilot could pass with no new demand at all.

The survey threshold has similar problems. A desk-offered survey reaches only visitors willing to stop. The question "could you have come at another time?" invites the answer that keeps the hours. The 25 percent bar also has no baseline.

The far-address metric has no threshold. It also cuts against the proposal's own rationale: Eastgate was chosen because residents can walk to it.

Finally, the 33 percent who wanted "weekend hours" were asked at a branch that already opens Saturdays. It is unclear whether they meant Sundays or longer Saturdays.

**Severity:** Significant weakness
**Test/mitigation:** Make the primary metric total weekend visits compared with the same weeks last year. Track how many distinct cardholders are new to weekend use. Give the far-address metric a threshold or drop it. Word the survey question neutrally.

The calendar doesn't fit the decision rule. If "the first Sunday in February" means February 7, 2027, the twelfth Sunday is April 25. The "final four weeks" the decision rests on are April 4–25. Unless the board meets in the last days of April, the April report will be missing some or all of the data the memo says matters most. Easter (March 28, 2027) also falls inside the window, and the memo doesn't say whether the branch opens that day.

Staffing per shift is unstated. At $310 for a four-hour opening, how many people work each Sunday? Does the $310 cover opening and closing time outside 1–5 p.m.? Is anyone working alone?

**Severity:** Significant weakness
**Test/mitigation:** Move the report to the May meeting, or start in mid-January. Specify staff per shift, the minimum safe staffing level, and the Easter plan. Add a small publicity line, for example notices at both complexes.

### The Sustainability Auditor: Can this be maintained indefinitely, or does it consume its own foundations?

User goal: Stress-test this proposal from several independent angles before it goes to the people who will decide on it.
Current task: Apply the Sustainability Auditor lens — Can this be maintained indefinitely, or does it consume its own foundations? — to this proposal.
Success criterion: A 100-200 word critique grounded in proposal specifics, with severity and a test/mitigation.

The pilot is cheap and well contained. The decision it leads to is neither. At $355 per Sunday, year-round Sunday hours cost about $18,460 a year. That is more than four times the pilot and well above the programming line's $11,800 balance, which drops to about $7,540 after the pilot. The memo names no recurring funding source. A "continue" vote in April would therefore commit money that hasn't been identified.

The same applies to staffing. Four volunteers covering twelve Sundays is a novelty commitment; fifty-two Sundays a year is a standing obligation. Volunteer goodwill typically fades, and two backups is a thin reserve.

The memo also doesn't say what the $4,260 would otherwise have funded in programming. Unspent money still has a purpose behind it.

**Severity:** Significant weakness
**Test/mitigation:** Require the April (or May) report to include an annualized cost, a named recurring funding source, and a staffing model that doesn't depend on volunteers. State what programming the pilot money displaces.

### The Power Analyst: Who gains power, and who loses it?

On balance this is a strength. The pilot is small, reversible and announced as temporary, so patrons can't claim they were misled if it ends.

The structural concern is that the Branch Services Manager proposes the pilot, sets the thresholds, collects the data and writes the recommendation. The board's only check is a report produced by the pilot's advocate.

For staff, premium shifts going to volunteers is fair for twelve weeks. A permanent schedule raises questions the staff agreement may govern. Who has first claim on premium shifts? Can non-volunteers be required to work Sundays? Staff who didn't volunteer have no voice in the pilot but carry the risk if it becomes permanent.

Among patrons, Eastgate's renters gain access. Residents near other branches are told only that the library might "try a different branch," with no stated basis for choosing one.

**Severity:** Point to consider
**Test/mitigation:** Write the thresholds into the board motion. Have raw counts reported alongside the recommendation. Consult the staff representative on assignment rules before any permanent schedule.

## Synthesis

All four personas answered `yes`, so no lens failed to engage.

### Convergent Findings
- **The decision the pilot feeds is underspecified.** The Sustainability Auditor (no funding source for continuing), the Implementation Engineer (decision data won't be ready in time) and the Power Analyst (criteria can drift in the proposer's own report) all point here. The memo carefully plans the trial but not what a "continue" vote commits the board to.
- **The success criteria may not measure what they claim.** The Empiricist shows the Saturday comparison rewards displacement and the survey leans toward approval. The Power Analyst adds that the same person sets and judges these criteria.

### Tensions
- **Rigor vs. cost.** The Empiricist's fixes (prior-year comparison, tracking new cardholders) and the Implementation Engineer's (a later report or earlier start) improve the evidence but add time or work. The memo's selling point is that the pilot is small and cheap.
- **Reversible vs. self-committing.** The Power Analyst counts the twelve-week notice as a strength. The Sustainability Auditor notes that a passing result creates pressure to continue without funding. Both are true: the pilot is easy to end, and success is harder to act on.

### Blind Spots
- **Systems Thinker (external, future):** The effects on Saturday staffing load and on demand at other branches were only partly covered, through the Empiricist's displacement point.
- **Security Analyst (external):** Sunday safety was not examined, including whether anyone works alone, emergency response, and building security with fewer people nearby.
- **End User Advocate (present, builder):** Sunday transit and walking conditions in February, and how residents of the two complexes will actually learn about the hours.
- **Historian:** Peer library systems have run Sunday pilots. Their attendance curves would ground the 60 percent and 25 percent thresholds, which currently look arbitrary.
- **Regulatory Mind:** The staff agreement's terms on premium-shift assignment were flagged but not examined.
- **Unverified premises:** The survey sample and representativeness, and the claim that Eastgate has the system's highest weekday visits, were not checked because no fact-check was run.
